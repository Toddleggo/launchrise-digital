# 2029–2031: Three Futures {.chapter number="5"}

Here's where honest writers have to change gear.

For 2027 and 2028 we can see the trend lines, the products being built and the money being spent, and we can make reasonable calls. By 2029 to 2031 the fog thickens. Small differences now (how fast the Doubling Clock keeps ticking, how quickly businesses actually adopt this stuff, what governments do, whether there's a recession, whether there's an unexpected breakthrough or an unexpected wall) compound into very different worlds.

So instead of pretending to know which world we'll get, I'm going to describe three. These are scenarios, not predictions. Think of a weather forecaster saying there's a 30 per cent chance of storms, a 50 per cent chance of showers and a 20 per cent chance of sunshine. You pack for all three and you keep an eye on the sky.

For each one I'll describe what daily life might feel like, who gets hit, where the opportunities are and, most usefully, the warning signs that would tell you it's the one we're heading into. Then I'll give you the moves that pay off whichever one turns up.

## Scenario one: the Slow Burn

AI keeps improving, but the jumps get smaller. Perhaps the Doubling Clock slows: the systems keep getting better on tests, but turning that into reliable real-world work proves harder than expected. Or perhaps the technology races ahead but businesses, as they usually do, adopt it slowly, held back by messy data, legal worries, cost, habit and plain old organisational inertia. Think how long it took offices to make proper use of computers. The technology was around for decades before most workplaces were transformed by it.

In this world, by 2031 most people use AI every day the way they use search engines now: for writing, research, planning, homework help and customer service. It's useful and unremarkable. Some jobs have shrunk (fewer junior admin roles, fewer call-centre staff, fewer entry-level coders), but new roles have appeared too, and the overall job market looks broadly like it did after previous waves of technology. The people hit hardest are the young and the entry-level in exposed office jobs, the pattern we can already see, continuing at roughly today's pace. The opportunities are in helping slow-moving businesses finally get AI working, and early adopters in every field pull steadily ahead.

You'd know you were in this scenario if unemployment stays roughly flat through 2028, if stories of AI disappointments start outnumbering stories of breakthroughs, if the young-worker gap in the payroll data stops widening, and if AI company revenues grow more slowly than their spending.

My honest feeling is that this is one of the more likely outcomes, but less likely than many sceptics assume.

## Scenario two: the Steady Climb

The trend lines roughly continue. The Doubling Clock keeps ticking, maybe a little slower, maybe not. By 2030 AI agents can reliably handle a large share of computer-based tasks that take a person a day or more. Businesses adopt them unevenly, fast in some industries and slow in others, but steadily. Robots begin moving from trials into real paid work in warehouses, factories and logistics, although general-purpose home robots are still mostly a novelty.

This is a big reshuffle rather than a collapse. Lots of people are doing different jobs from the ones they did in 2025. Many office jobs have been redesigned around AI, with fewer people each doing more, and for a lot of professionals the job has shifted from doing the work to directing, checking and owning the work. Unemployment may rise somewhat, particularly for certain groups, and some regions and industries are hit hard. There's political pressure for retraining schemes, stronger unemployment support and new rules. There's also a lot of new work: implementing AI, the trades powering the boom, care, and anything where a trusted human is the product.

The International Monetary Fund has warned that in most scenarios AI is likely to worsen inequality unless governments act. In this world, that shows. People who learned to direct AI early, and people who own assets, do very well. People who didn't adapt fall further behind. Those hit hardest are entry-level and mid-level office workers whose work was mostly processing information, some professionals whose value lay mainly in knowing things rather than judging, deciding or relating, and drivers in cities where autonomous vehicles expand. But the opportunities are very large. One person with the right skills and a few AI agents can run a business that used to need ten staff.

You'd know you were in this scenario if measurements show AI reliably completing multi-day tasks by 2028, if the young-worker gap keeps widening and starts showing up among mid-career workers too, if big companies report smaller workforces alongside growing profits, if governments start seriously debating income support linked to automation, and if robot deployments move from hundreds to many thousands.

I think this is the most likely of the three, but I hold that view loosely.

## Scenario three: the Fast Break

Something speeds up. Perhaps AI systems become good enough at AI research to help build their successors much faster. Perhaps there's a breakthrough in reliability, so agents go from right half the time to right almost every time on long tasks. Perhaps the heads of some AI companies, who have said they expect extremely capable AI before the end of this decade, turn out to be right.

In May 2025 Dario Amodei, chief executive of Anthropic, one of the leading AI companies, warned that AI could eliminate up to half of entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years and push US unemployment as high as 10 to 20 per cent. That's a serious person with inside knowledge making a serious warning. It's also one person's warning, not a consensus. By 2026 he was also stressing the other side of the ledger: that as AI makes work cheaper, demand for it may grow.

In this world, disruption is fast and widespread. Many kinds of office and knowledge work are substantially automated within a few years. Unemployment rises sharply among white-collar workers, a shock that, unlike most earlier waves of automation, hits the educated middle class hard. Governments scramble and emergency policies appear. Some things get much cheaper, and enormous wealth concentrates among those who own the AI systems. At the same time, science and medicine could speed up dramatically. Very good things and very disruptive things could happen side by side.

Broadly, anyone whose work is done mostly at a computer and mostly involves processing information would be exposed, including many professionals who currently feel safe. Physical, human, local and trusted work becomes relatively more valuable. So do owning assets and businesses rather than only selling your time, and being skilled at directing AI systems.

You'd know you were heading this way if AI systems start reliably doing week-long or month-long projects, if major breakthroughs announced by AI companies are confirmed by independent researchers, if graduate unemployment jumps sharply in a single year, if big companies announce very large cuts that stick rather than being walked back, and if governments launch emergency job programs or income support specifically tied to AI.

I think this is less likely than the other two, but it's not a fringe idea. Several of the people closest to the technology take it seriously. That's reason enough to prepare for it without planning your whole life around it.

## Wild cards

A few things could happen in any of the three scenarios, and I can't predict them and won't pretend to.

[Wild card]{.tag} A serious AI accident or misuse (a major cyberattack, a financial-market event, or an AI system behaving in a seriously harmful way) that triggers a big public and political backlash. A major recession unrelated to AI, which would hit jobs hard and muddy the picture. An energy or chip bottleneck: AI needs enormous amounts of electricity, and the International Energy Agency expects data-centre electricity use to roughly double by 2030, while the most advanced chips come from a small number of factories. Geopolitical conflict involving the countries and companies that make those chips. And general-purpose home robots arriving much sooner than expected. That's possible, but I wouldn't bet on it in this window.

## The no-regret moves

So how do you plan when you don't know which future is coming? You do the things that pay off in all three. Economists call these "no-regret moves".

The first is building a cash buffer. In the Slow Burn it's simple good sense. In the Steady Climb it buys you time to retrain. In the Fast Break it could be the difference between a stressful year and losing your home (Chapter 12).

The second is becoming genuinely good at directing AI. In the Slow Burn it makes you better than your peers, in the Steady Climb it's what employers pay for, and in the Fast Break it's one of the few skills whose value goes up (Chapters 14, 15 and 22).

The third is strengthening your human advantages: relationships, trust, hands-on skills, judgement and accountability. In every scenario these hold their value longer than pure information work (Chapter 6).

The fourth is building a second source of income, even a small one (Chapters 12 and 13). The fifth is protecting your family from scams, which are rising in every scenario (Chapter 16). The sixth is teaching your kids to think rather than just to answer (Chapter 19). And the seventh is staying informed, calmly. Check the warning signs once a quarter. Not daily, and not by doom-scrolling.

None of these requires you to know which future is coming. All of them make you stronger in any of them.

## Why three futures instead of one

I know it would be more satisfying if I just told you what's going to happen. Plenty of people will. Some will say it's all hype and nothing will change; others will say we're all doomed by 2029. Both camps will sell a lot of content.

But the right way to prepare for uncertainty isn't to guess the answer. It's to become someone who does well across the whole range of answers. That's what the rest of this book is for.

## Notes

- *The three scenarios.* The author's framework. Relative likelihoods are the author's judgement, not measured probabilities.
- *Dario Amodei's warning.* Reported by Axios, "Behind the Curtain: A white-collar bloodbath," 28 May 2025. His later emphasis on rising demand: *Fortune*, 5 May 2026.
- *IMF on inequality.* Kristalina Georgieva, "AI Will Transform the Global Economy. Let's Make Sure It Benefits Humanity," IMF Blog, 14 January 2024 (about 40 per cent of global employment exposed to AI, about 60 per cent in advanced economies; AI likely to worsen overall inequality in most scenarios).
- *Data-centre electricity.* International Energy Agency, *Energy and AI* (2025): global data-centre electricity consumption projected to more than double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, slightly more than Japan's total electricity consumption today.
- *Slow adoption of computers.* Often called the "productivity paradox" in economics; a historical framing, not a prediction.
