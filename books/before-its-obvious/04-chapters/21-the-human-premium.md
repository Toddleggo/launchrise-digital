# Chapter 21
## The Human Premium

I promised you at the start that this book wouldn't be depressing. So let's turn the corner.

Because here's something that doesn't get said nearly enough:

> **When machines make everything cheap, being human becomes a luxury.**

Not a luxury in the sense of "only for the rich." A luxury in the sense of *something people value more, seek out more, and will pay more for, precisely because it's no longer the default.*

I call this the **Human Premium**. And I think it's going to be one of the most important economic forces of the next decade.

---

### What happens when something becomes cheap

Think about what happened to a few things when machines made them cheap.

When factories made furniture cheap, handmade furniture didn't disappear. It became a premium product. People pay a lot for a table made by a real craftsperson.

When recorded music made music available everywhere for almost nothing, live music didn't die. Concerts became one of the biggest parts of the music industry. People pay enormous sums to be in the room with a real performer.

When mass production made bread cheap, the local sourdough bakery didn't vanish. In many towns there's a queue out the door on Saturday mornings.

The pattern is consistent: **when the machine version becomes abundant, the human version becomes special.** Not for everything — most people buy the cheap table and the supermarket bread most of the time. But for the things that matter to us, many people are willing to pay for the real, the local and the human.

AI is about to do this to a whole new set of things: writing, advice, design, customer service, teaching, companionship, entertainment. The machine version will become extraordinarily cheap and abundant.

Which means the human version of those things is about to become special.

---

### Remember Klarna

Back in Chapter 7, I told you about Klarna — the company whose AI assistant did the work of around 700 customer service agents, and which then started hiring humans again because quality dropped.

Here's the line from Klarna's CEO that I think will turn out to be quietly prophetic. He said it was critical that customers knew *"there will be always a human if you want."*

Think about that. A tech company, one of the most aggressive adopters of AI in the world, concluded that being able to reach a human was something customers *valued* — something worth promising.

I expect you'll see this everywhere in the next five years:

- Banks advertising "talk to a real person, any time."
- Doctors' practices promoting "you'll always see a human doctor."
- Schools highlighting small classes and real teachers.
- Brands labelling products "human-made," "human-written," "designed by people."
- Local businesses competing on "you'll deal with me, personally."

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — "Human" becomes a selling point in many industries as AI-generated goods and services become the default.

---

### Where the Human Premium will be strongest

The Human Premium isn't everywhere. People won't pay extra for a human to process their parking fine. It's strongest where one or more of these things are true:

**1. The stakes are emotional.**
Weddings, funerals, births, illness, divorce, grief. People want a person at the most important moments of their lives.

**2. Trust is essential.**
Money advice, legal help, medical decisions, caring for your children or parents. When the downside of getting it wrong is big, people want someone they can look in the eye and hold responsible.

**3. The experience is the product.**
Live music, sport, theatre, restaurants, travel, classes, retreats, tours. The point is being there, with people.

**4. Identity and meaning are involved.**
Art, craft, writing, style. People buy things that say something about who they are, and "a real person made this" carries meaning that "a machine generated this" doesn't.

**5. It's local and physical.**
Your hairdresser, your mechanic, your personal trainer, your local café. Relationship plus presence.

**6. It involves care.**
Nursing, aged care, childcare, disability support, therapy. The human is the care.

---

### How to position yourself for the Human Premium

Whether you're employed, self-employed or running a business, you can lean into this.

**Be visible as a person.**
In a world of faceless AI content, a real face, a real name and a real story stand out. If you run a business, show the people behind it. If you're a professional, let clients know who you are. Your personality is a competitive advantage now.

**Make "human" part of your offer — honestly.**
If you're a writer, "written by a person who's lived it" matters. If you're a tradie, "I'll turn up myself" matters. If you're a coach, "you'll have my phone number" matters. But be honest — using AI for admin and research doesn't make your work less human. Claiming "no AI involved" when that's not true would destroy the trust you're selling.

**Invest in relationships, not just transactions.**
Remember names. Follow up. Send the handwritten card. Ring when you said you'd ring. These small human acts are becoming rarer and more noticeable.

**Use AI to buy yourself *more* human time.**
This is the key. The smartest move isn't to choose between AI and the human touch. It's to use AI to clear away the admin, so you have *more* time for the human part. The tradie who uses AI for quotes has more time to chat with customers. The GP with an AI scribe has more time to look at the patient instead of the screen. The teacher who uses AI for marking has more time for the kid who's struggling.

> **Use the machine to do the machine work, so you can do more of the human work.**

**Get better at the human skills.**
Listening. Empathy. Explaining things clearly. Handling conflict. Making people feel looked after. These have always been valuable. They're about to become more valuable than ever, because they're the part of your work AI can't copy.

---

### A caution

The Human Premium is real, but it's not a guarantee for everyone.

It's most available to people who are genuinely good at the human part — and who can reach customers who value it. Human-premium goods and services often cost more, which means they're more accessible to people with money. In a downturn, many customers will choose the cheaper machine version.

So don't bet everything on it. Build it as one part of a resilient career — alongside AI skills, a cushion and a second income.

But if you've been worried that there's no room left for people in an AI world, I hope this chapter has helped you see the opposite. The more the machines can do, the more the things only humans can do will matter.
