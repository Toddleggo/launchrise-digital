# Chapter 8
## Health, Education, Law & the Licensed Professions

If you spent years training for your job — nursing school, teaching qualifications, law school, medical school, pharmacy, physio, social work — you might be reading this with a particular kind of worry.

*I gave my twenties to this. I've got the debt to prove it. Is it all about to be worth nothing?*

Here's the honest answer: probably not. But the job you trained for will change more than your training prepared you for. And the parts of it that change are, in many cases, the parts you liked least.

Let's go through it.

---

### Why licensed professions have a head start

Remember the Five Moats from Chapter 6: Hands, Heart, Trust, Taste and Accountability.

Most licensed professions have at least three.

A nurse has **Hands** (physical care), **Heart** (comfort, reassurance) and **Accountability** (a registration that can be taken away). A lawyer has **Trust**, **Taste** (judgement about strategy) and **Accountability** (professional duties to the court and client). A teacher has **Heart**, **Trust** and a room full of real children who need a real adult.

The licence itself is a moat. Society has decided that certain decisions need a named, qualified, responsible human. Changing that requires changing laws, regulations, professional standards and public trust — all of which move slowly. Very slowly.

That doesn't make you untouchable. It makes you *slower to touch*. Which means you've got time. The question is whether you use it.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — Licensed, hands-on and accountable professions see AI change the *work around the core* first — documentation, admin, research, drafting — while the core stays human through 2031.

## Healthcare

### What's already happening

One of the clearest early wins for AI in healthcare is the thing doctors and nurses complain about most: paperwork.

AI "scribes" listen to a consultation (with the patient's permission) and write up the notes. According to the 2026 Stanford AI Index, physicians using clinical AI tools reported spending **83% less time** writing notes.

Think about what that means. Many doctors spend a large part of their day on documentation. Giving that time back is giving time back to patients — or to doctors' own lives, which in many health systems are stretched to breaking point.

AI is also increasingly used to help read scans, flag abnormal results, check for drug interactions, summarise patient histories and triage symptoms. The research literature on AI in medicine has grown enormously.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — AI documentation tools are in real use. Diagnostic support tools are in use in some areas, with evidence still developing for many.

### Nurses

**Changing:** Documentation, handover notes, care plans, scheduling, medication checks.

**Protected:** Physical care. Assessing a patient in person. Noticing that something's "not right." Comforting frightened patients and families. Advocating for patients. Working in a team under pressure.

**Outlook:** Nursing is one of the most AI-resilient professions in this book. The physical, emotional and accountable core is enormous, and many countries face nursing shortages. AI is likely to take some paperwork off your plate. The nurses who do best will be comfortable using AI tools for documentation and monitoring, and will use the freed time well.

**Forecast: LOW exposure.**

### Doctors (GPs and specialists)

**Changing:** Note-taking. Reading test results and scans (with AI flagging). Research and keeping up to date. Writing letters and referrals. Initial triage.

**Protected:** Examining patients. Complex diagnosis across multiple conditions. Having difficult conversations. Making decisions under uncertainty. Taking responsibility. Procedures and surgery.

**Outlook:** The role shifts toward being a skilled interpreter and decision-maker who uses AI as a powerful second opinion — and toward the human conversation. Specialities focused mainly on pattern recognition in images (some parts of radiology and pathology) may see bigger shifts in how work is organised, though predictions that AI would replace radiologists have so far proven wrong. The Jagged Edge applies here powerfully: AI can be brilliant on common cases and dangerously confident on unusual ones.

**Forecast: LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure.**

### Pharmacists

**Changing:** Checking prescriptions for errors and interactions. Stock management. Routine information queries.

**Protected:** Counselling patients face-to-face. Clinical judgement on complex cases. Accountability. Vaccinations and in-person services.

**Outlook:** The dispensing and checking side is increasingly automated. The clinical and advisory side grows. Pharmacists who lean into consultation and clinical services do well.

**Forecast: MODERATE exposure.**

### Medical admin, billing and coding

**Outlook:** This is where healthcare's highest exposure sits. Medical coding, billing, prior authorisations, appointment booking and records management are rule-based information tasks. If you work here, treat Chapter 7's advice on admin and claims as yours too, and look at moving toward patient-facing coordination roles or clinical support training.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure.**

### Allied health (physio, OT, speech therapy, psychology, dental)

**Outlook:** Largely physical and relational. AI helps with notes, exercise programs, assessments and admin. Talk-based therapies face a particular question — AI chatbots offering mental health support — which I cover in Chapter 17. My view: AI tools may widen access to basic support, but the trusted human relationship at the heart of therapy remains hard to replicate, and the risks of getting it wrong are serious.

**Forecast: LOW exposure (physical); LOW-TO-MODERATE (talk-based).**

## Education

### What's happening with students

This is where AI has moved fastest in everyday life. According to the Stanford AI Index 2026, about **80% of American high school and college students** use AI for school. Only about **6% of teachers** said they had clear policies on it.

Read those two numbers again. That's an entire generation working out how to use a powerful tool largely on their own, while the adults responsible for their education try to catch up.

### What AI can do for learning

It can be extraordinary. Two careful studies, published in 2025:

**Nigeria:** A World Bank randomised trial gave secondary students six weeks of after-school English tutoring using an AI assistant (Microsoft Copilot, built on GPT-4), guided by teachers. The learning gains were equivalent to roughly **one and a half to two years** of normal schooling. The gains showed up in end-of-year exams too.

**Harvard:** Physics students using a carefully designed AI tutor learned **more than twice as much** as students in an active-learning class run by experienced instructors — and in less time.

The critical detail in both: the AI was *designed and supervised to make students think*, not to give them answers. In the Harvard case, the tutor was built on proven teaching principles and specifically set up not to just hand over solutions.

Without that design, the same tools can let students skip the thinking entirely. I call that the **Lazy/Smart Fork**, and it's the centre of Chapter 19.

### Teachers

**Changing:** Lesson planning. Creating worksheets and resources. Marking routine work. Writing reports. Differentiating material for different levels. Admin.

**Protected:** Managing a classroom. Building relationships with kids. Noticing the child who's struggling at home. Motivating the unmotivated. Being a role model. Pastoral care. Teaching kids *how* to think, and how to use AI wisely.

**Outlook:** I'm optimistic here. Teachers are drowning in admin, and AI can take a lot of it. The job likely shifts toward being a coach, mentor, guide and designer of learning — with AI tutors helping individual students practise between lessons. The teachers who struggle will be those who try to ignore AI or ban it outright; students will use it anyway.

A teacher who becomes excellent at designing AI-supported learning — who knows how to use AI to make students think harder rather than less — is going to be in demand.

**Forecast: LOW exposure for classroom teachers; MODERATE for tutoring, online course creation and some lecturing roles.**

### Tutors

A special mention, because this is a genuinely exposed area. Basic homework help and exam-prep tutoring is something AI does cheaply and at any hour. Tutors who survive will offer what AI can't: accountability, motivation, a relationship, working with kids who have particular needs, and teaching families how to use AI well.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure.**

## Law

### What's happening

Law is made of documents, and AI is very good with documents. Legal research, contract review, summarising cases, drafting standard letters and agreements — AI tools are already widely used in larger firms.

But law has also produced some of the most famous AI failures. Lawyers in several countries have been caught submitting court documents that cited cases the AI made up. Judges have imposed penalties. It's a perfect illustration of the Jagged Edge: AI writes a convincing legal argument, and some of it is fiction.

### Lawyers

**Changing:** Legal research. Document review. Drafting standard contracts and letters. Due diligence. Summarising.

**Protected:** Advising clients on strategy. Negotiation. Advocacy in court. Judgement on novel situations. Client relationships. Professional accountability.

**Outlook:** The traditional law firm model — lots of junior lawyers billing hours for research and document review, a few senior partners doing strategy and client work — is under real pressure. The bottom of that pyramid is exactly the work AI does well. Expect fewer junior roles, more pressure on hourly billing, and more value in judgement and relationships.

There's a new-demand story here too. Legal help is too expensive for most ordinary people. If AI makes it much cheaper, far more people might get legal help who currently go without. That could create a lot of work for lawyers willing to serve everyday clients efficiently.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for junior/research-heavy roles; MODERATE for experienced advisory and court work.**

### Paralegals and legal secretaries

**Outlook:** Among the most exposed roles in law. Move toward client-facing coordination, managing AI legal tools, specialist areas like conveyancing oversight or compliance, or retraining toward areas with more human contact.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure.**

## Other licensed and professional roles

### Engineers (civil, structural, mechanical, electrical)

AI helps with calculations, drafting, design options and documentation. The engineer who signs off that a design is safe — and who understands the physical world, the site, the regulations and the risks — remains central. **LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure.**

### Architects

AI can generate designs and visualisations quickly. The architect's value moves toward understanding clients, sites, regulations, buildability and taste. **MODERATE exposure.**

### Financial advisors

AI can build portfolios and answer questions. Trust, behavioural coaching ("don't sell everything in a panic") and life planning remain human. **MODERATE exposure.**

### Social workers, counsellors, clergy, community workers

Deeply human work built on Heart and Trust. AI helps with admin and case notes. **LOW exposure.**

### Police, emergency services, defence

Physical, accountable, high-stakes and human. AI will be used heavily for analysis and surveillance — which raises its own serious questions (Chapter 20). **LOW exposure** for frontline roles.

### The trap for professionals

Here's the risk I most want professionals to hear.

It's not that AI replaces you. It's that **a professional who uses AI well replaces a professional who doesn't.**

A GP who uses an AI scribe sees more patients, with better notes, and goes home on time. A lawyer who uses AI research tools produces better advice faster and charges less. A teacher who uses AI to personalise learning gets better results.

If you're a professional and you're waiting for your professional body to tell you how to use AI, you'll be waiting a while, and colleagues who didn't wait will pull ahead.

---

### The path for professionals

**1. Use AI on your admin now.** Documentation, letters, research, summaries. Get your time back. Within professional and privacy rules — check your organisation's policies, and never put identifiable patient or client information into tools that aren't approved for it.

**2. Learn where the Jagged Edge is in your field.** Your professional knowledge is what lets you catch AI's mistakes. That's increasingly what you're paid for.

**3. Lean into the human core.** The conversation, the judgement, the physical skill, the accountability. That's your moat. Get even better at it.

**4. Become the bridge.** Every profession needs people who understand both the professional work and the AI tools. Be one of them. Help write your organisation's AI policy. Lead the trial.

---

> ### DO THIS NOW
>
> **1. Find out your organisation's AI policy.** If there isn't one, that's useful information too — and an opportunity to help write one. (One email)
>
> **2. Use an approved AI tool on one piece of admin** this week — a letter, a lesson plan, a summary — without including any private personal information. (15 minutes)
>
> **3. Write down three mistakes AI would likely make in your field** that only a trained professional would catch. That's your value, on paper. (10 minutes)
