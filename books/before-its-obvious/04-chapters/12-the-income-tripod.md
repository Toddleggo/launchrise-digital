# Chapter 12
## The Income Tripod

Let's talk about the thing that actually keeps people awake.

Not "will AI become conscious?" Not "will robots take over?"

*Can I pay the rent if something happens to my job?*

That's the real fear under most of the AI anxiety I hear about. It's not abstract. It's the mortgage, the kids' school shoes, the car loan, the groceries that cost more every month. It's the knot in your stomach when you look at your bank balance two days before payday.

So this chapter isn't about AI at all, really. It's about making your household strong enough that whatever AI does — slow, steady or fast — you have *time* and *choices*.

Because that's what financial resilience really is. Not being rich. Having time and choices when things change.

*(A quick, necessary note: I'm not a financial advisor, and this chapter is general education, not personal advice. Your situation is yours. For big decisions — especially about debt, investing, tax or retirement — talk to a qualified, independent professional.)*

---

### Why a table needs three legs

Picture a stool with one leg. You can balance on it, if you're careful and nothing moves. Most households live like this: one main income (or two incomes from the same kind of work), and nothing much underneath.

Knock that one leg, and you're on the floor.

Now picture a stool with three legs. Knock one, and it wobbles — but it stands. You've got time to fix the broken leg.

**The Income Tripod** is the idea that every household, over the next five years, should be working toward three legs:

**Leg 1: Your main income** — your job or main business. Made as secure as possible (Parts III and V of this book).

**Leg 2: A skill-based second income** — something that earns money from a *different* source than your main job, even if it's small. (Chapter 13.)

**Leg 3: A cash cushion** — money set aside that buys you time if Leg 1 or Leg 2 breaks.

You don't need to build all three this month. Most people won't have all three solid for a year or two. But knowing that this is the shape you're building toward changes every financial decision you make.

Let's start with the leg that matters most, and that most people skip.

## Leg 3: The Cash Cushion

### Why it matters more now

When a job disappears, the damage isn't just the lost income. It's the *speed*. If you have no savings, you have to take the first thing that comes along — even if it's worse paid, a worse fit, or a job that's also about to be automated. You can't afford to retrain. You can't afford to wait for the right thing. Panic makes decisions for you.

A cushion changes that. Three months of expenses saved means that if your job goes, you can breathe, look properly, maybe take a short course, and make a *good* decision rather than a desperate one.

In every one of the three futures from Chapter 5, a cushion helps. In the Fast Break future, it could be the most important thing you did.

### How big?

The standard advice from most financial educators is to aim for **three to six months of essential expenses**. Not income — *essential expenses*: rent or mortgage, food, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, transport.

If that number looks impossible, I understand. For a lot of families it is, right now. So here's a ladder:

1. **$500–$1,000** — enough to stop a car repair or a dental bill from becoming debt.
2. **One month of essential expenses.**
3. **Three months.**
4. **Six months** — especially if you're in a high-exposure job, self-employed, or the only earner.

Each rung makes you meaningfully safer than the one below it. Don't be discouraged by the top rung. Climb the next one.

### How to actually build it

- **Automate it.** Set up an automatic transfer to a separate savings account on payday, even a small amount. What you don't see, you don't spend.
- **Keep it separate and boring.** A high-interest savings account at a different bank from your everyday account works well for many people — easy enough to get to in an emergency, awkward enough that you won't dip into it for takeaway.
- **Feed it windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses, birthday money, side income — a chunk goes straight to the cushion until it's full.
- **Don't invest your emergency cushion in things that can crash.** The point is that it's *there* when you need it. Shares and crypto can fall exactly when the economy — and your job — is under pressure.

## The Vulnerability Audit

Before you can strengthen your household, you need to know where it's weak. Answer these honestly. Tick each one that applies.

**Income vulnerability**
☐ More than 80% of our household income comes from one job.
☐ Two incomes, but both in high-exposure jobs (Task Test score over 40% Automate).
☐ My main income is in an industry already shrinking.
☐ I have no income source outside my main job.

**Cost vulnerability**
☐ Our fixed costs (rent/mortgage, loans, subscriptions) are more than 60% of take-home pay.
☐ We have high-interest debt (credit cards, payday loans, buy-now-pay-later).
☐ We'd have to sell something important if we lost one income for three months.

**Cushion vulnerability**
☐ We have less than one month of expenses saved.
☐ Our savings are all in investments that could fall in value.

**Protection vulnerability**
☐ We don't have income protection or life insurance, and people depend on us.
☐ We haven't checked what unemployment support we'd be entitled to.

**Skill vulnerability**
☐ I haven't learned a significant new skill in two years.
☐ I don't use AI in my work at all.

**Score:** Count your ticks.
- **0–3:** Solid foundation. Focus on building Leg 2.
- **4–7:** Some real weak spots. Pick the top two and start there.
- **8+:** You're exposed. Not doomed — exposed. Start with the cushion and high-interest debt, and work through this chapter step by step.

### Fix the leaks: high-interest debt

If you have credit card debt, payday loans or buy-now-pay-later balances charging high interest, that debt is quietly working against you every month.

In a period of uncertainty, high-interest debt is dangerous for a simple reason: if your income drops, the debt doesn't. It keeps growing.

A common approach financial educators recommend:
1. Build a small starter cushion first ($500–$1,000), so emergencies don't create *new* debt.
2. Then throw everything extra at the highest-interest debt while paying minimums on the rest.
3. Once that's gone, roll the payment onto the next.

If debt is overwhelming, speak to a free, non-profit financial counselling service. Most countries have them. They're genuinely helpful and they won't judge you.

---

### Lower your fixed costs (the underrated move)

People focus on earning more. But in uncertain times, **lower fixed costs are a form of freedom.**

Every dollar of fixed monthly costs you remove is a dollar you don't need to earn, every month, forever. It lowers the income you need to survive, which means more jobs become viable, more career changes become possible, and a smaller cushion covers more months.

Go through your bank statement line by line. Ask of every regular payment: *would I sign up for this again today?*

(AI can help. Export your last three months of transactions — with account numbers removed — and ask an AI to categorise them and find recurring charges. Many people find subscriptions they'd forgotten about.)

## If unemployment rises: the household downturn plan

This is the plan you write *now*, while things are calm, so you're not writing it in a panic.

Sit down with your partner, or on your own, and write answers to these:

**1. Early warning signs for our household.** What would tell us our income is at risk? (Hiring freeze at work. Restructure announced. Team not being replaced. Industry news. Your Task Test score.)

**2. What we'd cut first.** List the costs you'd cut immediately if income dropped. Decide now, so it's not an argument later.

**3. What we'd protect.** Housing, food, kids' essentials, insurance. In that order.

**4. Who we'd call.** People who might know of work. Recruiters. Former colleagues. Your network (Chapter 7 told you to start building it).

**5. What we're entitled to.** Look up now what unemployment benefits, redundancy entitlements and hardship assistance exist where you live. Your employment contract. Any income protection insurance you have.

**6. What we'd do in the first week.** Update your CV and portfolio. Apply for benefits immediately. Contact your lender early if you might struggle with repayments (many have hardship arrangements, and talking early helps). Start the 30-day challenge (Chapter 15) if you haven't.

Write it on one page. Put it somewhere you'll find it. Hope you never need it.

## What about Universal Basic Income?

You'll hear a lot about **Universal Basic Income** — the idea that governments could give everyone a regular payment, no strings attached, especially if AI takes a lot of jobs.

It's worth understanding what the evidence says, so you can think clearly about it.

The largest recent study in the US was run by a non-profit called OpenResearch, backed by Sam Altman, the head of OpenAI. For three years, 1,000 lower-income people received **$1,000 a month**, no strings attached. A comparison group of 2,000 received $50 a month.

What happened?

- Recipients worked **slightly less** on average — but the researchers found evidence this was about having more choice (for example, being pickier about jobs, or spending time on family), not about people not wanting to work.
- They were **more likely to report business ideas** and plans to start a business.
- Stress and food insecurity improved noticeably — but mainly **in the first year**, and those improvements faded.
- There were **no significant effects on physical health**.
- Parents spent more on their children.

So: not a miracle, not a disaster. A modest, meaningful help for people on low incomes.

Would governments introduce something like this if AI caused widespread job losses? Possibly. Serious people from across the political spectrum have argued for versions of it. Others argue strongly against it on cost and other grounds. Other ideas — stronger unemployment insurance, retraining accounts, shorter working weeks, taxes on automation — are also being debated.

> **FORECAST LABEL: POSSIBLE** — New forms of income support are introduced in some countries in response to AI disruption by 2031.

My honest advice: **don't plan your family's future around it.** If support arrives, great. It'll be a floor, not a lifestyle, and it'll likely arrive after the pain rather than before. Build your own tripod first.

## A word on investing (briefly and carefully)

People ask: *should I invest in AI companies?*

I'm not going to tell you what to buy. But here are some principles that financial educators generally agree on, and that apply especially now:

- **Your cushion comes first.** Don't invest money you might need in the next few years.
- **Don't put all your eggs in the same basket as your job.** If you work in tech and all your investments are tech shares, one bad year could hit your job *and* your savings at the same time.
- **Be very wary of hype.** When everyone's talking about getting rich from the latest thing, be careful. The dot-com bubble made some people rich and wiped out many others — even though the internet itself turned out to be huge. (Chapter 1.)
- **Be extremely wary of "AI trading bots" and investment schemes.** According to the FTC, investment scams were the single biggest category of reported fraud losses in the US in 2025 — around $7.9 billion. Many use AI buzzwords. (Chapter 16.)
- **Get independent advice** for big decisions — from someone who isn't paid commission on what they sell you.
