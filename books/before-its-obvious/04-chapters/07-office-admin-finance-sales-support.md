# Chapter 7
## Office, Admin, Finance, Sales & Support

If you work at a desk, on a computer, processing information for most of the day, this chapter is for you. I'm going to be straight with you, because you deserve it more than you deserve comfort.

This is where AI is hitting first.

Not because office workers are less skilled. Because the work happens entirely on a screen, in words and numbers, which is exactly the material these systems are made from. The intern who's read everything is most at home in your inbox.

But "hitting first" doesn't mean "wiping out." And the story of what's happening is more interesting — and more hopeful for the right people — than most headlines suggest.

Let's start with the best real-world evidence we have.

---

### What happened when 5,000 support agents got AI

In a study later published in one of the most respected economics journals in the world, economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond followed 5,179 customer support agents at a large software company as the company rolled out an AI assistant. It suggested responses to customers in real time, and the agents could use them or not.

The results:

- On average, agents resolved about **14% more issues per hour**.
- For newer and less-skilled agents, the improvement was about **34%**.
- For the most experienced, top-performing agents, it made very little difference.
- Customers were *nicer* to agents using it. Agents were less likely to quit.

Think about what that means.

The AI had effectively learned how the best agents talked to customers — and passed those habits to everyone else. A new starter could perform like someone with months more experience.

That's wonderful news for a new starter. It's also a quiet warning. If a new person with AI can perform like an experienced one, a company may decide it doesn't need as many experienced ones. Or as many people at all.

Both of those things can be true. And that's the office work story in a nutshell.

---

### The Klarna lesson: AI-first, then human again

Klarna is a Swedish payments company — the "buy now, pay later" button you see at online checkouts.

In early 2024, Klarna announced that its AI assistant, built with OpenAI, had handled 2.3 million customer conversations in its first month — work the company said was equivalent to about **700 full-time agents**. It resolved issues in under two minutes on average, compared with eleven for humans. Klarna said it expected the assistant to add around $40 million in profit that year.

It was held up everywhere as proof that AI could replace call centres.

Then, in 2025, Klarna's CEO, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, said something unusual for a tech boss. He told Bloomberg that cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" and that the result was "lower quality." Klarna started hiring humans for customer service again.

It didn't rip out the AI. The AI still handles the bulk of simple queries. But Klarna brought people back for complex and sensitive cases, and said it was critical that customers always know they can reach a human if they want to.

This is one of the most important stories in this book, and I'll come back to it in Chapter 21. Here's what it tells office workers:

> **AI can do a huge amount of the routine work. It can't — yet — do the difficult, emotional, high-stakes or unusual work as well. And customers notice the difference.**

The jobs that remain are the ones that handle what the AI can't. So that's the work you want to be excellent at.

## Role-by-role breakdowns

For each role, I'll show which tasks are most exposed, which are protected, and a practical path forward. Use these alongside your own Task Test from Chapter 6.

---

### Administrative Assistants, Office Managers & Executive Assistants

**Most exposed (Automate):** Scheduling and calendar juggling. Taking and typing meeting notes. Drafting routine emails and letters. Booking travel. Filing and document formatting. Data entry.

**Changing (Assist):** Preparing reports and presentations. Researching suppliers or options. Managing projects. Preparing budgets.

**Protected (Anchor):** Being the trusted right hand who knows how the boss thinks and what matters to them. Managing relationships and office dynamics. Handling sensitive and confidential situations. Solving the physical problems of an office. Being the calm person in a crisis.

**The path forward:** The best assistants will become **operations coordinators** — people who run the AI tools that handle the scheduling and admin, and spend their time on the judgement, relationships and problem-solving. Learn to set up automations (Chapter 14). Learn basic project management. Become the person in your organisation who makes the AI tools actually work.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for routine admin roles; MODERATE for trusted EAs and office managers.**

---

### Bookkeepers, Accounts Staff & Accountants

**Most exposed:** Data entry. Bank reconciliation. Invoice processing. Accounts payable and receivable chasing. Standard tax returns. Standard financial reports. Expense checking.

**Changing:** Preparing complex returns. Financial analysis and forecasting. Audit preparation. Month-end processes.

**Protected:** Advising clients on real decisions. Complex tax planning. Handling audits and disputes with the tax office. Understanding a specific business deeply. Being the trusted advisor who can say "don't do that."

**The path forward:** Two strong directions. First, become an **advisor**: move from compliance to advice, from "here's your tax return" to "here's what I'd do if I were you." Small businesses are crying out for part-time finance directors who understand their business; AI lets you serve far more of them. Second, become an **AI finance operations specialist**: the person who sets up and manages automated finance systems for businesses. Get certified in the main accounting software platforms and their AI features.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for data entry and junior bookkeeping; MODERATE for accountants who shift toward advice.**

---

### Customer Service & Call Centre Staff

**Most exposed:** Answering common questions. Password resets, tracking orders, changing details. Routine complaints. Following scripts.

**Changing:** Complex troubleshooting (AI suggests solutions, human leads). Written responses (AI drafts, human edits).

**Protected:** Angry, upset or vulnerable customers. Complex, unusual problems. High-value customers. Situations needing discretion — breaking rules to do the right thing. Anything involving real empathy.

**The path forward:** The call centre of 2030 is likely to have fewer people, handling harder calls, for better pay — and some people who *manage* AI systems: checking what they're getting wrong, improving their responses, dealing with what they escalate. Aim to be one of those. Get good at the hardest calls. Put your hand up to help test and improve your company's AI tools. Consider moving toward **customer success** (helping business customers get value from a product), which is more relationship-based.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for tier-one support; MODERATE for complex and specialist support.**

---

### Sales

**Most exposed:** Researching prospects. Writing cold emails. Updating the customer database. Writing proposals from templates. Basic follow-ups. Scheduling meetings.

**Changing:** Preparing for meetings. Analysing which leads are worth chasing. Writing personalised proposals.

**Protected:** Building real relationships. Reading a room. Handling objections in person. Negotiating. Complex, high-value sales where trust matters. Understanding what a customer *actually* needs, not what they say they need.

**The path forward:** AI is a gift to good salespeople. It clears away the admin that eats most of a salesperson's week and lets them spend more time with people. The ones who struggle will be those whose value was mainly sending lots of emails. Lean into the human side: relationships, consultative selling, deep knowledge of your customer's industry. Use AI for every scrap of prep work.

Also watch: selling in a world of AI-generated sales emails. When every inbox is flooded with perfectly written, personalised automated emails, the salesperson who picks up the phone, turns up in person or gets a warm introduction stands out more, not less.

**Forecast: LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure for relationship sales; HIGH for high-volume transactional sales.**

---

### Human Resources

**Most exposed:** Writing job ads. Screening CVs. Scheduling interviews. Answering routine policy questions. Onboarding paperwork. Payroll admin.

**Changing:** Designing training. Analysing staff surveys and data. Writing policies.

**Protected:** Handling conflict, grievances and disciplinary matters. Supporting staff through personal crises. Making difficult calls about people. Building culture. Deciding who to hire.

**Note on hiring:** When AI helps with hiring decisions, it's one of the areas lawmakers consider "high risk." Under the EU AI Act, stricter obligations for AI used in hiring are due to apply from December 2027. Being the person who understands how to use AI in HR *fairly and legally* is valuable.

**The path forward:** Move toward **people partnering** — advising managers on handling real situations — and toward **workforce transition**, which will be a big area as companies restructure around AI. Someone has to help organisations retrain and redeploy their people well.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure for HR admin; LOW-TO-MODERATE for HR advisory roles.**

---

### Marketing Coordinators & Content Roles

(Covered in more depth in Chapter 10, but briefly:)

**Most exposed:** Writing social posts, blog posts, product descriptions and ad variations. Basic image creation. Reporting on campaign numbers.

**Protected:** Understanding customers deeply. Strategy. Brand judgement. Knowing what will actually land with *these* people. Relationships with partners and media.

**The path forward:** Move from *making* content to *directing* it. One marketer with AI can produce what a small team used to. The value shifts to deciding what's worth saying and to whom.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for content production; MODERATE for strategy and brand roles.**

---

### Insurance, Claims, Banking Back-Office & Data Processing

**Most exposed:** Processing standard claims. Checking documents. Data entry. Compliance checks against rules. Loan and application processing for straightforward cases.

**Protected:** Complex and disputed claims. Fraud investigation. Dealing with vulnerable customers — someone whose house just burned down. Decisions requiring human discretion and accountability.

**The path forward:** This is one of the most exposed areas in this book, because it's rule-based work on documents. If you're here, don't wait. Move toward complex claims, investigation, fraud (which is growing — see Chapter 16), customer advocacy or compliance oversight of AI systems. Regulators will increasingly require humans to check AI decisions about people's money. Be one of those humans.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure.**

---

### Project Managers & Team Leaders

**Most exposed:** Writing status reports. Tracking tasks. Scheduling. Taking minutes. Updating project plans.

**Protected:** Leading people. Resolving conflict. Making trade-off decisions. Managing stakeholders. Taking responsibility when things go wrong.

**The path forward:** Your job shifts toward managing a team that includes both people and AI agents. That's a new skill almost nobody has yet. Get ahead of it. (Chapter 22.)

**Forecast: LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure.**

---

### The three things every office worker should do this year

Whatever your specific role, three moves apply to everyone in office work:

**1. Become the AI person on your team.**
Not the tech expert. Just the person who's figured out how to use it well for your team's actual work, and can show others. In every workplace I can think of, this person becomes more valuable, more visible and harder to let go.

**2. Move toward the hard stuff.**
Volunteer for the complex cases, the difficult clients, the messy problems. The routine work is what's going. The difficult work is what's staying. Build your reputation there.

**3. Build relationships outside your company.**
If your job does shrink, the thing that gets you your next one isn't your CV. It's the people who know what you're good at. Start investing in those relationships now, while you don't need them.
