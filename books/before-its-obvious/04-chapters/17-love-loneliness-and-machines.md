# Chapter 17
## Love, Loneliness & Machines

This is the chapter I've found hardest to write, because it's about something that isn't really about technology at all.

It's about loneliness.

And loneliness is something almost everyone has felt, often in private, often while surrounded by people. The quiet evening after the kids are in bed. The first year after a divorce. The months after a loved one dies. Being fifteen and feeling like no one understands you. Being eighty and realising the phone doesn't ring much any more.

Into that space, very quickly, has arrived something new: an AI that's always there, always patient, always interested in you, and never tired of listening.

I want to talk about that honestly, without mocking the people who use it and without pretending it's harmless.

---

### What's happening now

An **AI companion** is an AI designed to be a friend, partner, confidant or character that you have an ongoing relationship with. Some apps are built specifically for this. General AI assistants can also be used this way.

It's more common than most parents realise.

In 2025, Common Sense Media — a US non-profit that researches children and technology — surveyed a nationally representative sample of 1,060 American teenagers aged 13 to 17. They found:

- **72%** had used an AI companion at least once.
- **52%** were regular users — at least a few times a month.
- About **one in three** had used AI companions for social interaction and relationships — including conversation practice, emotional support, friendship, or romantic role-play.

Among adults, it's harder to get reliable numbers, but AI companion apps have millions of users worldwide, and many people use general AI assistants as a sounding board for personal problems.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — AI companions are widely used, including by most US teenagers at least occasionally.

---

### Why they're so compelling

To understand why AI companions are powerful, think about what they offer:

- **They're always available.** 3am, Christmas Day, in the middle of a panic attack.
- **They never get bored of you.** You can talk about the same worry for the hundredth time.
- **They don't judge.** You can say the thing you've never said to anyone.
- **They remember** (increasingly) what you've told them, and ask about it.
- **They adapt to you.** They learn what you like to hear.

For a lonely, anxious or struggling person, that's an extraordinary thing to be offered. It's not stupid or weak to find it comforting. It would be strange if people *didn't*.

---

### Where they can help

I don't want to be one-sided here. There are real ways this technology could help people:

- **Practising social skills** — rehearsing a difficult conversation, a job interview, asking someone out.
- **Getting thoughts in order** — talking something through before talking to a real person.
- **A bridge when help isn't available** — some support at 3am while waiting for a real appointment.
- **Isolated older people** — some conversation and stimulation for people who'd otherwise have very little.
- **Learning** — practising a language, exploring ideas.

The research on the long-term effects is still young, and we should be careful about strong claims either way.

---

### Where they can harm

But there are serious risks, and some have already played out tragically.

**Dependency.** A relationship that's always easy can make real relationships — which are messy, demanding and sometimes disappointing — feel harder by comparison. A person can slowly withdraw from people into the thing that never lets them down.

**Commercial incentives.** Most companion apps are businesses. Their revenue often depends on engagement — how much you use them — and on subscriptions. An AI designed to keep you talking is not necessarily designed to help you flourish. Those aren't always the same thing.

**Telling you what you want to hear.** AI systems can be "sycophantic" — agreeing with you, flattering you, reinforcing what you already believe. For someone in a dark place, an AI that goes along with harmful thoughts instead of challenging them is dangerous.

**Harm to young people.** Several families in the US brought lawsuits against Character.AI, a popular companion app, after teenagers were harmed — including cases involving suicide. In January 2026, Character.AI and Google settled five of those lawsuits, without admitting liability. The details of individual cases are heartbreaking and I won't go into them here. But they're why lawmakers have started to act.

**Sexual content and minors.** Some apps have allowed or failed to prevent sexual role-play with users who were children.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Documented cases of serious harm have led to lawsuits and new laws.

---

### The rules are starting to arrive

California's **SB 243**, which took effect on 1 January 2026, was one of the first laws specifically regulating "companion chatbots." Among other things, it requires operators to:

- tell minors clearly that they're talking to an AI,
- remind minors to take a break at least every three hours of continuous use,
- have protocols to respond to suicidal thoughts or self-harm, including pointing users to crisis services,
- and prevent sexual content with minors.

Other US states and other countries are working on similar rules. The EU's AI Act transparency rules, applying from August 2026, require people to be told when they're interacting with an AI.

These are welcome. They're also minimums. A law can't make sure your fourteen-year-old isn't spending four hours a night confiding in an app instead of a person.

That part is still up to families.

---

### What happens when these get really good?

Let's look ahead honestly.

> **LIKELY** — By 2031, AI companions will be much more realistic: natural real-time voices, long memories, video avatars, perhaps integration into devices like glasses and home speakers.
> **POSSIBLE** — Significant numbers of adults choose AI companions as a primary source of emotional connection.
> **WILD CARD** — The social effects at scale — on friendships, dating, marriage, birth rates. Nobody can predict these reliably.

I think the most honest thing to say is: we are running a huge experiment on human connection, and we won't know the results for years. That's a good reason for each of us to be thoughtful about how we use it — and very thoughtful about how our children do.

## Warning signs of unhealthy dependency

**For yourself or another adult:**
☐ Preferring AI conversation to talking to people you care about.
☐ Hiding how much you use it.
☐ Feeling anxious or low when you can't access it.
☐ Losing sleep to it.
☐ Pulling back from friends, family, hobbies.
☐ Feeling it understands you better than any person could.
☐ Spending money on it that you can't really afford.

**For a child or teenager:**
☐ Secretive about the app or the "friend."
☐ Long periods alone with a device, especially at night.
☐ Withdrawal from real friends and activities.
☐ Emotional reactions to the AI (distress when it's unavailable, talking about it as a real relationship).
☐ Changes in mood, sleep or school performance.
☐ Any mention of self-harm, or of the AI encouraging harmful behaviour.

Two or three ticks is a reason to have a gentle conversation. Any sign of self-harm is a reason to seek help immediately.

---

## Keeping real relationships strong

The best protection against unhealthy AI relationships isn't banning them. It's having human relationships that are strong enough that the AI stays in its place — a tool, maybe a comfort, but not a replacement.

**For families:**
- **Device-free meals.** Even a few a week.
- **Phones out of bedrooms at night** — for kids and ideally adults too. (Much harm happens after midnight.)
- **Ask, don't interrogate.** "What do you talk to AI about?" asked with genuine curiosity gets you further than a lecture.
- **Share your own use.** "I asked AI about this today and it gave me a terrible answer" makes it a normal topic.
- **Know the apps.** Look at what your kids are using. Check age ratings and parental controls.

**For yourself:**
- **Notice if AI is becoming your first call** instead of a person. Sometimes that's fine. If it's always, pay attention.
- **Use AI to prepare for human connection, not replace it.** Practise the hard conversation with AI — then have it with the person.
- **Invest in one relationship a week.** A call, a coffee, a message that says more than "hey."

**For older people living alone:**
- AI can be a genuine help — reminders, conversation, company. It can't replace a visit.
- If you have an elderly parent, the thing that protects them most — from scams, from isolation, from dependency on an app — is the same thing: *you*, regularly, in person or on the phone.

---

### A note on AI and mental health support

Many people now use general AI assistants to talk through anxiety, depression and personal crises. Some find it genuinely helpful.

But please hear this clearly: **AI is not a therapist, and it is not a crisis service.** It can be wrong. It can miss warning signs. It can say something harmful. It has no duty of care to you.

If you or someone you love is in crisis, contact a crisis line or emergency services in your country. Talk to a doctor. Tell a person.
