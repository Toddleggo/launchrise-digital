# Chapter 14
## How to Actually Use AI

Most people who "tried AI and didn't see the fuss" made the same mistake.

They treated it like a search engine. They typed four words — *"write marketing email"* — and got back something bland and generic, and thought: *is that it?*

That's like hiring the most well-read intern in the world, shouting three words at them from across the room, and being disappointed they didn't read your mind.

This chapter is the practical how-to. You don't need to be technical. You need to learn to *brief* well, *check* well, and *build habits*. By the end, you'll be using AI better than most people who use it every day.

---

## Part 1: Talking to AI — the CRAFT method

The single biggest improvement most people can make is to give AI more to work with. Here's a simple way to remember what to include. I call it **CRAFT**.

**C — Context.** What's the situation? Who are you? What's the background?
**R — Role.** Who should the AI act as? ("You're an experienced bookkeeper." "You're a patient maths tutor for a 12-year-old.")
**A — Ask.** What exactly do you want?
**F — Format.** How do you want it? (A list? An email? A table? Under 200 words? Plain English?)
**T — Test.** Ask it to check its own work, point out weaknesses, or ask you questions before it starts.

### Bad prompt
> Write a marketing email.

### CRAFT prompt
> **Context:** I run a small family-owned bakery in a country town. We're launching gluten-free bread on Saturdays starting next month. Most of our customers are over 50 and have been coming for years. They like that we're local and personal.
> **Role:** Act as an experienced small-business copywriter who writes warm, plain, friendly emails — not salesy or corporate.
> **Ask:** Write an email announcing the gluten-free bread to our mailing list.
> **Format:** Under 150 words. A friendly subject line. No exclamation marks overload. Sign off from "Maria and the team."
> **Test:** Before you write it, ask me any questions you need answered to make it better.

The second prompt takes a minute longer to write. The result will be dramatically better.

### Five more habits that make a huge difference

**1. Have a conversation, not a transaction.** The first answer is a draft. Reply: *"Make it warmer."* *"Shorter."* *"That second paragraph doesn't sound like me — here's how I'd say it."* *"Give me three completely different versions."*

**2. Show it examples.** Paste in something you've written before and say *"Write it in this style."* Paste a report you liked and say *"Use this structure."*

**3. Ask it to interview you.** *"I need to write a business plan. Ask me questions one at a time until you have what you need."* This is one of the most powerful and underused techniques there is.

**4. Ask for options, then choose.** *"Give me ten headline options."* Your taste is the value. Let it generate; you decide.

**5. Ask it to critique.** *"What's weak about this?"* *"What would a sceptical customer say?"* *"What have I missed?"* AI is often better at criticising than at creating.

## Part 2: Research and fact-checking — the Two-Source Rule

AI is a brilliant research *starting point* and a dangerous research *ending point*.

Remember: it can be confidently wrong (Chapter 2). So here's the rule:

> **The Two-Source Rule: If it matters, don't trust it until you've confirmed it from two independent, reliable sources that aren't the AI.**

"Matters" means: money, health, legal, safety, anything you'll publish, anything you'll put your name to, anything you'll tell your kids is true.

### How to research well with AI

1. **Use AI to map the territory.** *"Explain the main options for [problem], the pros and cons of each, and what questions I should be asking."*
2. **Ask for sources — then check them exist.** Many AI tools can now search the web and link to sources. Click the links. Read the actual source. Make sure it says what the AI claims. (AI has been known to cite real sources that don't say what it claims, or sources that don't exist.)
3. **Go to primary sources for important facts.** Government websites, official statistics, the actual study, the actual law, the company's own announcement.
4. **Ask the AI to argue the other side.** *"What's the strongest argument against this?"* It helps you spot where you're only hearing one view.
5. **Be extra careful with numbers, dates, names, quotes and legal or medical specifics.** These are where errors hide.

## Part 3: What never goes into an AI

Before we go further — privacy. Treat anything you type into an AI tool as something that might be stored and potentially seen by others, unless you've checked the settings and the company's policies carefully.

**Don't put in:**
- Passwords, PINs or security answers.
- Full bank or credit card details.
- Government ID numbers.
- Other people's private information without their permission — especially customers', patients' or clients'.
- Confidential work information, unless your employer has approved the specific tool for that purpose.
- Anything you'd be devastated to see leaked.

**Do:**
- Check your AI tool's privacy settings, including whether your conversations are used to train future models, and switch that off if you prefer.
- Use your employer's approved tools for work.
- Remove names and identifying details before asking for help ("Client A," "my colleague").

## Part 4: Learning with AI

AI can be the best tutor you've ever had — *if* you use it to make you think, not to think for you.

Remember the Harvard and Nigeria studies (Chapter 8)? The AI tutors that worked were designed to make students work things out. You can set that up yourself:

> "I want to learn [topic]. Don't give me the answers. Teach me step by step, ask me questions to check I understand, and only move on when I get it right. Start by asking what I already know."

Other ways to learn with AI:

- **"Explain it like I'm 12, then like I'm a university student."** Two levels of understanding in two minutes.
- **"Quiz me."** After reading anything, ask it to test you.
- **"What are the five most common misunderstandings about this?"**
- **"Make me a 30-day learning plan for [skill], 20 minutes a day, with free resources."**
- **Practise conversations.** "Pretend to be a tough interviewer for a [job] role." "Pretend to be an angry customer; I'll practise handling you." "Let's practise Spanish ordering in a café."

## Part 5: Everyday productivity

Some of the most useful everyday uses, to get you started:

- **Email:** Paste in a long email thread: "Summarise what's being asked of me and draft a reply."
- **Meetings:** Record (with permission) and have AI write notes and action points.
- **Planning:** "Here's everything I need to do this week. Help me prioritise and schedule it."
- **Decisions:** "I'm deciding between X and Y. Ask me questions to help me think it through."
- **Difficult messages:** "Help me write a kind but firm message to [situation]."
- **Documents:** "Summarise this contract in plain English and list anything I should be worried about." (Then check anything important with a professional.)
- **Money:** "Here are my spending categories for three months. Where could I save?" (With account details removed.)
- **Family admin:** Meal plans with a budget. School holiday schedules. Birthday party plans.

## Part 6: Data and spreadsheets

You don't need to be a spreadsheet wizard any more.

- Upload a spreadsheet (without sensitive data) and ask: *"What are the three most interesting things in this data?"*
- *"Make me a chart showing sales by month."*
- *"Write me a formula that [does this]."* Then paste it into your spreadsheet.
- *"Find the rows where something looks wrong."*

Always sense-check the answers. AI can miscalculate or misread data. If a number looks surprising, check it by hand.

## Part 7: Automation — making things happen without you

This is where you move from "AI helps me" to "things happen automatically."

**Automation tools** (there are several popular no-code ones — search for "no-code automation tools" and check current reviews) let you connect apps together with simple rules: *when this happens, do that.* Many now include AI steps.

Examples anyone can set up:

- **When** a customer fills in my website form → **AI** drafts a personalised reply → **send** it to me to approve.
- **When** I get an invoice by email → **save** the attachment to a folder → **add** a line to my spreadsheet.
- **When** someone books an appointment → **send** a reminder the day before.
- **Every Monday** → **AI** summarises last week's sales and emails it to me.

Start with one small automation that saves you ten minutes a week. Then another. They add up.

## Part 8: AI agents

As we covered in Chapter 4, agents are AI that can take a goal and carry out multiple steps — browsing the web, filling in forms, using apps, writing and running code.

In 2026, many major AI tools offer some form of agent or "do it for me" mode. They're powerful and improving fast. They also make mistakes, so:

**Rules for using agents safely:**
1. **Start with low-stakes tasks.** Research, drafting, organising. Not paying bills or sending emails to important clients.
2. **Watch what it does** the first few times.
3. **Don't give it access to money, passwords or important accounts** without careful thought and strong safeguards.
4. **Require approval** before anything goes out into the world (emails sent, purchases made, posts published).
5. **Check the result** like you'd check a new employee's first week of work.

Try: *"Research the five best-reviewed [service] providers in [my area], compare their prices and reviews, and give me a table."* Watch how it works.

## Part 9: Building simple things with AI (yes, you)

This is the part that surprises people most.

You can now build simple apps, websites and tools without knowing how to code, by describing what you want to an AI. People call this "vibe coding" — you describe the vibe, the AI writes the code.

Try this:

> "Build me a simple web page I can open in my browser that works as a [chore chart for my kids / tip calculator / quote calculator for my cleaning business / habit tracker]. Make it colourful and easy to use on a phone. Give me the complete code in one file and tell me exactly how to open it."

Many AI tools will now build and show it to you directly. Others will give you a file to save and open.

Will it be perfect? No. Will you be amazed that you built something? Very likely.

**Be careful:** anything handling payments, passwords, personal data or anything important needs a real developer to check it. AI-written code can have security holes.

## Part 10: The habits of people who are really good at this

After everything above, here's what separates people who are genuinely good with AI:

1. **They use it every day,** for small things. Fluency comes from repetition.
2. **They brief it well,** with context.
3. **They iterate,** treating the first answer as a draft.
4. **They check what matters,** every time.
5. **They know where it's weak** in their own field.
6. **They keep thinking.** They use AI to go further, not to stop.
7. **They share what they learn** with others.

> **Use AI to think faster. Never use it to stop thinking.**
