# Chapter 16
## Scams, Voices & Fakes

If you only read one chapter of this book, I'd want it to be this one.

Not because it's the most important thing AI will do to the world. But because it's the thing most likely to hurt someone you love *this year* — and because the defence is so simple, so cheap and so fast that it would be a tragedy not to set it up.

By the end of this chapter, you'll have a family plan that would have stopped most of the scams I'm about to describe. It'll take you about thirty minutes.

---

### A video call where everyone was fake

In early 2024, a finance worker at the Hong Kong office of Arup — the British engineering firm behind the Sydney Opera House — received a message that appeared to come from the company's UK-based chief financial officer. It asked for a secret transaction.

The employee was suspicious. That's worth saying. They did what you'd hope someone would do: they were cautious.

Then they were invited to a video call. On the call were the CFO and several other colleagues — people the employee recognised, whose faces and voices they knew.

Every single one of them was a fake. Generated by AI.

Reassured by seeing and hearing people they trusted, the employee made fifteen transfers, totalling around **US$25 million**, to five bank accounts.

Arup confirmed the incident publicly in May 2024. Hong Kong police investigated. As of the latest reports I could find, the money had not been recovered.

Here's why I tell this story first. This wasn't a gullible pensioner clicking a dodgy link. This was a professional at a global company, who was *already suspicious*, who was overcome by the one thing we've always trusted most: seeing and hearing people we know.

If it can happen to them, it can happen to your mum. It can happen to you.

---

### The scale of it

Let me give you the numbers, because they're staggering.

In 2025, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center:

- Americans reported losing **$20.9 billion** to online crime — up 26% in a single year.
- People aged **60 and over** reported losing **$7.75 billion** — up **59%**.
- Cryptocurrency-related complaints accounted for more than **$11 billion** in losses.
- For the first time, the FBI's annual report included a section specifically on **AI-related complaints** — over 22,000 of them, with losses of nearly $900 million.

The Federal Trade Commission, counting differently, recorded **$15.9 billion** in reported fraud losses in 2025 — a record — including **$3.5 billion** lost to impostor scams (people pretending to be someone they're not).

And these are only the *reported* losses. Most scams are never reported. People are embarrassed. They blame themselves. The FTC has estimated that once under-reporting is taken into account, the true cost of fraud to American consumers in 2024 could have been as high as around $196 billion.

These are American figures because the US publishes the most detailed data. But the same patterns are being reported by police and consumer agencies around the world.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Online fraud is at record levels and growing fast, especially against older people. AI is now a recognised factor.
> **LIKELY** — AI-enabled fraud grows substantially through 2031.

---

### How AI changes the con

Scams aren't new. What AI changes is three things:

**1. Scale.** A scammer used to be able to work a handful of victims at once. AI lets one criminal run thousands of personalised conversations at the same time — on messaging apps, dating sites, email, social media.

**2. Quality.** The old giveaways — bad spelling, weird grammar, clumsy English — are gone. AI writes perfect, natural messages in any language, in any tone.

**3. Impersonation.** This is the big one. AI can now copy a person's voice from a short recording, and increasingly their face on video. That video of your grandson on Instagram, that voicemail greeting, that clip of you at a wedding — any of it could be enough raw material.

### The scams to know about

**The family emergency call (voice clone)**
Your phone rings. It's your daughter's voice — crying, panicked. "Mum, I've been in an accident. I'm in trouble. I need money now. Please don't tell Dad." Maybe another voice comes on: a "police officer" or "lawyer" who explains how to send the money.

It sounds exactly like her. Because it was made from her voice.

A 2023 McAfee survey of over 7,000 people across seven countries found that about a quarter had experienced an AI voice scam or knew someone who had — and of those who were targeted, a large majority said they lost money. (It's a survey by a security company that sells protection, so treat the exact figures with some caution. But the pattern has been confirmed by police warnings in many countries since.)

**The boss / CFO request (business email and video)**
Like Arup. A message or call from someone senior asking for an urgent, confidential payment or change of bank details.

**The investment and "romance" scam**
Someone befriends you online — on social media, a dating app, even a wrong-number text. Over weeks or months they build trust. Eventually, they introduce an investment opportunity, often cryptocurrency, often with an "AI trading platform" showing fake profits. Investment scams were the single biggest source of reported fraud losses in the US in 2025, at around $7.9 billion according to the FTC.

**The fake official**
Your bank, the tax office, the police, a delivery company, Medicare, a utility. Urgent problem. Must act now. Must pay or verify your details.

**Fake customer support**
You search online for help with your bank or an app, and the top result, or the number you call, is a scammer's.

**Sextortion and fake images**
Criminals use AI to create fake intimate images of people — including teenagers — from ordinary photos, and then threaten to share them unless paid. This is one of the most distressing crimes on the rise, and it has driven young people to self-harm. More on protecting kids from this in Chapter 19.

### The three red flags that almost every scam shares

Here's the good news. However sophisticated the technology gets, nearly every scam still needs the same three things to work:

> **1. URGENCY — "Now. Right now. There's no time."**
> **2. SECRECY — "Don't tell anyone. Don't call anyone."**
> **3. UNUSUAL PAYMENT — gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, new bank details, cash couriers.**

When you see two or more together, stop. It's almost certainly a scam, no matter how real the voice or the face.

Why these three? Because scammers need to stop you from doing the one thing that defeats them: **checking**. Urgency stops you thinking. Secrecy stops you asking someone. Unusual payment makes the money impossible to get back.

## The Family Defence Plan

Here's the simple system. It works whether the voice is real or fake, because it doesn't depend on you being able to tell the difference. (You often can't.)

### Rule 1: The Family Safe Word

Choose a word or short phrase that only your close family knows. Something that wouldn't appear on social media and couldn't be guessed. Not a pet's name, not a street, not a birthday.

*"Purple wombat." "Uncle Kevin's boat." "Pancake Tuesday."*

Anyone in the family who calls asking for money or help in an emergency must be able to say it. If they can't, you hang up and call them back on the number you already have.

Tell everyone: *"If I ever call you in a panic asking for money, ask me for the word. If I can't say it, it's not me. I promise I won't be offended."*

Write it down somewhere safe (not in your phone notes). Don't text it. Say it in person or on a call you initiated.

### Rule 2: Hang Up, Call Back

If anyone — family, bank, boss, police, tax office — calls asking for money or personal details, **hang up and call them back on a number you already know is real.** Not the number they give you. Not the number in the text. The number on the back of your bank card, on the official website you typed yourself, or in your contacts from before.

Real banks, real police and real family will not mind. Scammers will.

### Rule 3: The 24-Hour Money Rule

Agree as a family: **no unexpected transfer over a set amount (say $500) happens without waiting 24 hours and talking to one other family member first.**

Almost no genuine emergency requires money within minutes that can't wait for a phone call to someone else. Nearly every scam does.

### Rule 4: Never Pay With Those

Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers to strangers, and handing cash to a courier are the scammer's favourite payment methods, because they're nearly impossible to reverse. No real government agency, bank or police force will ask you to pay with gift cards. Ever.

### Rule 5: Lock Down the Basics

- **Turn on two-step verification** (a code sent to your phone or an authenticator app) for email, banking and social media.
- **Use a password manager** so every account has a different, strong password.
- **Set social media to private,** and think twice about posting videos with lots of clear speech — especially from children.
- **Ask your bank** what protections they offer: transfer limits, alerts, a "trusted contact" who can be notified of unusual activity, and delays on new payees.
- **Consider a call-blocking or scam-screening setting** on your phone, if your provider offers one.

---

### Talking to your parents (without insulting them)

This is the conversation many adult children dread. Older people can hear "you might get scammed" as "you're getting old and stupid."

So don't frame it that way. Try this:

> "Mum, Dad — I just read about a scam where criminals copy someone's *voice* using AI and call their family pretending to be in trouble. They used it to steal $25 million from a big engineering company, and the person on the call was a professional who was already suspicious. I'm worried someone might copy *my* voice and call you. Can we set up a family password, so if I ever call needing money, you'll know it's really me?"

Notice what that does. It makes *you* the one at risk of being copied. It makes them the protector. It shows it can happen to professionals. And it gives them a simple action.

Then, together:
- Choose the safe word.
- Write down "Hang up, call back" on a card by the phone.
- Put the bank's real number next to it.
- Agree the 24-hour rule.
- Offer to help them set up two-step verification.

Check in every few months. Scams evolve. A short "anything weird happen lately?" goes a long way.

---

### If it happens

If you or someone you love is scammed:

1. **Act immediately.** Contact your bank straight away — minutes can matter. Ask them to stop or recall the payment.
2. **Report it** to the police and your national fraud reporting agency (in the US, the FBI's IC3 and the FTC; other countries have their own).
3. **Change passwords** on any accounts that might be affected.
4. **Beware of "recovery" scams.** Scammers often target victims a second time, promising to get their money back for a fee.
5. **Be kind.** To yourself or your loved one. Shame keeps people silent, and silence helps criminals. These are professional criminals using advanced technology. Being fooled is not a character flaw.

---

### What's coming

I'd expect the tools scammers use to keep getting better and cheaper: real-time voice conversion (talking live in someone else's voice), real-time video faces, AI systems that run scam conversations automatically in dozens of languages.

At the same time, defences are improving: banks using AI to spot unusual transactions, phone networks flagging likely scam calls, rules requiring AI-generated content to be labelled (the EU's rules started applying in August 2026).

But the most powerful defence won't be technology. It'll be habits. The family that has a safe word and a hang-up-call-back rule is protected against almost any version of this con, however good the fake gets.

That's why I'd ask you to do this tonight.
