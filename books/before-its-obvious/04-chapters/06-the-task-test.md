# Chapter 6
## The Task Test

"Will AI take my job?"

It's the question everyone asks. And I'm going to tell you something that might sound annoying at first: it's the wrong question.

Not because it doesn't matter. Because it can't be answered. A job isn't one thing. It's a bundle of dozens of different tasks, tied together with a job title and a pay slip.

A receptionist doesn't "do reception." A receptionist answers phones, books appointments, greets people, calms down upset customers, handles deliveries, notices when the regular client looks unwell, reminds the boss about the meeting they've forgotten, knows which supplier to ring when the printer breaks, and a hundred other things.

AI doesn't come for jobs. It comes for **tasks**. One at a time. Some fall quickly. Some fall slowly. Some don't fall at all.

So the useful question isn't "will AI take my job?" It's:

> **"Which of my tasks can AI do, which can it help me do, and which need a human — and what happens to my job when that mix changes?"**

That question *can* be answered. And by the end of this chapter, you'll have answered it for yourself, on paper, in about twenty minutes.

---

### The six ways AI can change a job

Before we test your job, it helps to know what we're testing for. When AI touches a job, it can do six quite different things. People lump them together, and that's where a lot of confusion comes from.

**1. AI replaces a task.**
One thing you used to do, you don't do anymore. A bookkeeper used to type in receipts; now the software reads them. The job still exists; one task is gone.

**2. AI changes the job.**
Enough tasks shift that the job looks different. A marketing coordinator who used to spend most of their week writing copy now spends most of it choosing, editing and testing AI-written copy — and talking to customers to work out what to say.

**3. AI reduces the number of people needed.**
Each person can do more, so the company needs fewer of them. Nobody's job disappears exactly — there are just fewer of those jobs. This is the "hiring freeze nobody announced" from Chapter 1.

**4. AI creates new demand.**
This is the one people forget. When something gets cheaper, people often want more of it. When spreadsheets arrived, bookkeepers worried. What actually happened was an explosion in financial analysis, because suddenly you could run numbers that were impractical before. If AI makes legal help cheaper, perhaps many more people get legal help. If it makes software cheaper, perhaps every small business gets custom software. That can create *more* work, not less.

**5. Robots replace physical tasks.**
A different track with a different timeline. Driving, warehouse picking, some factory work. Slower and more expensive than software, but real.

**6. New jobs appear.**
Jobs that didn't exist before: people who build and manage AI agents, people who check AI output in regulated industries, people who help businesses set it all up, people who design how humans and AI work together. We can't list them all yet, the same way nobody in 1995 could have listed "social media manager."

When you look at your own job, all six might be happening at once. Some tasks disappearing. The job changing shape. Fewer people doing it. More demand in some areas. New roles popping up next door.

That's why we test tasks, not jobs.

---

### The A-A-A Test

Every task you do falls into one of three buckets.

**AUTOMATE** — AI can do this task, now or within a couple of years, with little human input. A human might glance at the result, but the work itself is done by the machine.

*Examples: typing up meeting notes; basic data entry; first-draft product descriptions; sorting emails; simple scheduling; translating a standard document; routine code.*

**ASSIST** — AI can make you faster or better at this task, but a human still needs to lead it, shape it, check it or finish it.

*Examples: writing a report; preparing a quote; researching a problem; analysing figures; planning a lesson; drafting a legal letter; diagnosing a fault from a customer's description.*

**ANCHOR** — This task needs a human, and is likely to for years. It's anchored in the physical world, in human relationships, in trust or in responsibility.

*Examples: fixing a leaking pipe; comforting a scared patient; negotiating with an angry client; deciding whether to hire someone; signing off a building as safe; teaching a nervous kid to swim; being the person who says "I'll take responsibility for this."*

---

### The Five Moats

How do you know if a task is an Anchor? Look for the Five Moats. A moat is the water around a castle — the thing that makes it hard to get in. These are the five things that make a task hard for AI to take.

**1. HANDS — It happens in the physical world.**
Does the task need someone to be physically present, to touch, lift, fix, build, clean, carry or move things in unpredictable places? The physical world is hard for machines, especially outside controlled environments like factories. Your kitchen, a building site, an old house with dodgy wiring — these are still very difficult for robots.

**2. HEART — It depends on human connection.**
Does the task depend on people *wanting* to deal with another human? Comfort, care, motivation, persuasion, trust built over years. A grieving family doesn't want a chatbot. A nervous first-time home buyer wants a person they trust.

**3. TRUST — People need to know who's behind it.**
Does the task depend on a real, known person or relationship? Your reputation in your local area. The client who's been with you for twelve years. The customer who comes back because they like *you*.

**4. TASTE — It needs judgement about what's good.**
Does the task require knowing what's right, what fits, what works for *this* situation, *this* client, *this* brand? AI can produce a thousand options. Someone still has to know which one is right and why. That comes from experience.

**5. ACCOUNTABILITY — Someone has to be responsible.**
Does the task require someone who can be held responsible if it goes wrong? A licence, a signature, a legal duty, a professional reputation on the line. Society doesn't let software sign off on a bridge, prescribe medication without oversight or take the blame in court. That's likely to remain true for some time — and in some areas, it may become *more* true, as regulations require a human in the loop.

A task with **none** of these moats is likely to be automated sooner or later.

A task with **one** moat is safer, but that moat might get crossed.

A task with **two or more** moats is your strongest ground. Build your future there.

### The Worksheet

Get a piece of paper, or open a note on your phone. This takes about twenty minutes, and it's the most useful twenty minutes in this book.

**Step 1: List your tasks.**
Write down fifteen things you actually do in your job. Be specific. Not "customer service" — "answer customer emails about late orders." Not "admin" — "update the job tracking spreadsheet."

If you're stuck, think through a typical week, day by day. What did you do on Monday morning? Tuesday afternoon?

**Step 2: Estimate the time.**
Next to each task, write roughly what percentage of your working week it takes. They should add up to about 100%. It doesn't have to be exact.

**Step 3: Sort them.**
Mark each task **AU** (Automate), **AS** (Assist) or **AN** (Anchor).

If you're not sure, ask yourself:
- Could a smart intern who'd read everything do this from a computer, with clear instructions? → probably **AU**
- Could that intern do a decent first draft, but I'd have to fix it or finish it? → probably **AS**
- Does it need my hands, my relationships, my judgement or my signature? → probably **AN**

**Step 4: Count the moats.**
For each Anchor task, write which of the five moats protect it: Hands, Heart, Trust, Taste, Accountability.

**Step 5: Work out your Exposure Score.**
Add up the time percentages:
- Total % of time on AU tasks = ___
- Total % of time on AS tasks = ___
- Total % of time on AN tasks = ___

Here's how to read it:

| If your Automate % is… | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Under 20%** | **Low exposure.** Your job is mostly protected by moats. AI will change your admin, not your core. Focus on using AI to clear the boring stuff and grow your Anchor work. |
| **20–40%** | **Moderate exposure.** Your job will noticeably change. You'll likely be expected to do more in the same time. Get good at AI now, and shift your time towards Assist and Anchor work. |
| **40–60%** | **High exposure.** A significant chunk of what you're paid for could be done by AI in the next few years. Your job may change a lot, or there may be fewer of them. Start actively building new skills this year. |
| **Over 60%** | **Very high exposure.** Your role, as currently designed, is at serious risk of shrinking or disappearing. This isn't a verdict on *you* — it's a verdict on the task mix. Your experience is valuable; it needs a new home. Chapter 11 is your next stop. |

**Step 6: The Future Job Description.**
Now write down what your job would look like if all the AU tasks disappeared and the AS tasks took half the time. What's left? What would you fill the freed-up time with?

That's the job you want to be growing into. Because that's the job that will still exist.

### Worked Example 1: Medical Receptionist

Let's run the test on a medical receptionist at a busy GP practice.

| Task | Time % | A-A-A | Moats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answering phones and booking appointments | 25% | AU | — |
| Checking in patients at the desk | 10% | AS | Heart |
| Processing payments and billing queries | 10% | AU | — |
| Updating patient records and forms | 10% | AU | — |
| Handling repeat prescription requests | 5% | AS | Accountability |
| Calming upset or distressed patients | 10% | AN | Heart, Trust |
| Noticing when someone seems very unwell and alerting a nurse | 5% | AN | Heart, Accountability |
| Managing the waiting room | 5% | AN | Hands, Heart |
| Chasing test results and referrals | 10% | AS | — |
| Coordinating doctors' schedules and sorting clashes | 5% | AS | Taste |
| General admin, filing, scanning, emails | 5% | AU | — |

**Automate:** 25 + 10 + 10 + 5 = **50%**
**Assist:** 10 + 5 + 10 + 5 = **30%**
**Anchor:** 10 + 5 + 5 = **20%**

That's **high exposure**. AI phone systems that can book appointments already exist. Billing and records are going the same way.

But look at what's left. The human face of the practice. The person who notices the elderly man looks grey and gets a nurse. The one who calms the mother whose child has a fever. The one who knows the regulars.

A smart receptionist in this position could grow into a **patient experience coordinator** — someone who handles the human side, manages the AI systems that do the booking, deals with anything the systems can't, and makes sure vulnerable patients don't fall through the cracks. That job likely pays more and is more secure. But it only goes to the person who can operate the new systems *and* is great with people.

### Worked Example 2: Accountant at a Small Firm

| Task | Time % | A-A-A | Moats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data entry and bank reconciliation | 20% | AU | — |
| Preparing standard tax returns | 20% | AS→AU | Accountability |
| Preparing financial statements | 10% | AS | Accountability |
| Answering routine client emails | 10% | AS | — |
| Advising clients on business decisions | 10% | AN | Trust, Taste, Accountability |
| Tax planning for complex situations | 10% | AS | Taste, Accountability |
| Meeting clients, building relationships | 10% | AN | Heart, Trust |
| Dealing with tax office audits and disputes | 5% | AN | Accountability, Trust |
| Supervising and training junior staff | 5% | AN | Heart, Taste |

**Automate:** 20% (plus a good chunk of the 20% on standard returns over time)
**Assist:** 50%
**Anchor:** 30%

Moderate-to-high exposure — but with a clear path. The number-crunching shrinks. The *advising* grows. An accountant who uses AI to do their compliance work in a fraction of the time, and spends the freed-up hours being a proper advisor to small businesses — a sort of part-time finance director — becomes more valuable, not less.

The risk is for the *junior* accountant whose entire job was the data entry and standard returns. That rung of the ladder is the one wobbling.

---

### What if your score scared you?

Take a breath.

A high score doesn't mean you'll be unemployed next year. It means the *design* of your job is exposed. You aren't your job design. You're a person with experience, relationships, knowledge of your industry and the ability to learn.

Look again at your Anchor tasks. Look at your Assist tasks. That's where your future is. The goal over the next two years is to move as much of your working time as possible from the top of the list to the bottom — from Automate to Assist to Anchor.

And if your job really is almost all Automate? Then you've just found out *early*, which is the whole point of this book. You now have time to move deliberately, rather than being pushed suddenly. Chapter 11 will show you where people with your kind of experience can go.

---

### What if your score seems too low?

Be a little suspicious of yourself.

Remember the Jagged Edge and the perception trap from Chapter 3. People are generally bad at judging how much AI can do, in both directions. We tend to think *our* work is special. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it's more routine than we'd like to admit.

A good check: for every task you marked as Anchor, ask an AI assistant to try it — or to explain how it *would* do it. If it does a surprisingly good job, move that task up a category.

And one more check: think about the Doubling Clock. Your score today is a snapshot. If the length of work AI can handle keeps doubling, some Assist tasks become Automate tasks. Look at your Assist list and ask: *which of these could it do alone in three years?*

---

### The real lesson of the Task Test

Here's the thing I most want you to take from this chapter.

The people who get hurt by technological change are rarely the people who saw it coming. They're the people who assumed their whole job would either be fine or be gone, and so did nothing.

The truth is almost always in between. Some of your tasks will go. Some will get easier. Some will become more important than ever.

**The whole game is to move toward the tasks that are becoming more important — before you're forced to.**

The next five chapters go industry by industry, so you can see how this plays out in your world specifically, and where people like you can go next.

>
> **1. Do the worksheet.** Fifteen tasks, time percentages, A-A-A, moats, Exposure Score. Twenty minutes. Don't skip this one. (20 minutes)

> **2. Write your Future Job Description.** Three or four sentences: what your job looks like when the Automate tasks are gone. (5 minutes)
>

### Research Notes — Chapter 6

- **Tasks vs jobs approach** — consistent with the task-based framework used in economics research (e.g., Autor, Levy & Murnane, 2003; Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock, "GPTs are GPTs," 2023/2024) and in the Anthropic Economic Index, which classifies AI use at the task level. — framework
- **Automation vs augmentation distinction** — Anthropic Economic Index (Jan–Jun 2026): ~52% augmentation vs ~45% automation on Claude.ai; Stanford *Canaries*: employment declines concentrated where AI automates rather than augments. — **NOW**
- **Spreadsheet / new demand example** — widely cited historical example of technology increasing demand for related work; illustrative. Also referred to as the Jevons paradox in economics.
- **A-A-A Test, Five Moats, Exposure Score thresholds** — author's frameworks; thresholds are rules of thumb, not empirically derived cut-offs.
- **Worked examples** — illustrative task breakdowns; percentages are typical estimates, not measured data. AI phone booking systems for medical practices are commercially available as of 2026.
