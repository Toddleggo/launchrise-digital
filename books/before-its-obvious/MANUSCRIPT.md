I can't fix that by telling you to calm down. And I can't fix it by telling you to panic. What I can do is show you the evidence, tell you plainly how solid it is, and then hand you something to *do* about it.

That's the promise.

Here's what I'm asking for: your patience with uncertainty.

Nobody knows exactly what the next five years hold. Not the people building these systems. Not the economists. Not me. Anyone who tells you precisely what 2031 looks like is selling something. But "nobody knows exactly" isn't the same as "nobody knows anything." We know a lot. We can see which direction the wind is blowing and roughly how hard. We can prepare for a storm without knowing the exact minute it arrives.

So throughout this book, I'm going to label things the way a good weather forecaster would.

### The Four Forecast Labels

**NOW** — This is happening. It's documented. I'll tell you where the evidence comes from.

**LIKELY** — Strong evidence or broad agreement among serious people points this way. Not certain. Worth planning around.

**POSSIBLE** — Credible people argue for it, but the evidence is thin, or experts genuinely disagree. Worth knowing about. Not worth losing sleep over.

**WILD CARD** — It could happen. Nobody can reliably predict whether or when. I'll mention these so you're not blindsided, and then I'll move on.

When you see a number in this book, it came from somewhere real, and the notes at the end of each chapter tell you where. When I'm guessing, I'll say I'm guessing. When the experts are fighting, I'll show you both sides of the fight.

# Chapter 1
## The Hiring Freeze Nobody Announced

Nobody got fired.

That's the part people miss.

When we imagine machines taking jobs, we picture something loud. A factory floor going quiet. A boss with a cardboard box. A news crew outside the gates, a union rep with a megaphone, a politician promising to fix it.

What's actually happening is quieter than that. And in some ways it's worse, because you can't see it.

Someone in accounts leaves to have a baby, and nobody replaces her. Her work gets split between two colleagues and "the new system." A law firm that used to take on eight graduates takes three. A marketing agency stops advertising junior roles at all. Nobody makes an announcement. There are no layoffs. A door that used to be open just quietly shuts.

You'd never notice it from the inside. The people who still have jobs keep them. The office looks the same. The Christmas party still happens.

The only people who notice are the ones standing outside the door, knocking, wondering why nobody answers.

---

### What the payroll records show

In August 2026, a team of economists at Stanford University — Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen — published an update to a study they'd first released a year earlier. They'd been given access to payroll data from ADP, one of the largest payroll processors in America. Real pay slips. Millions of them. Not surveys, not opinions. Who was actually getting paid, for what job, at what age.

They were looking for one thing: is AI already changing who gets work?

Here's what they found.

**First: there's no economy-wide jobs collapse.** Most people's work looked the way it always had. If you've been told that AI has already wiped out millions of jobs, that's not what the paychecks say.

**Second: one group is falling behind.** Young workers, aged 22 to 25, in the jobs most exposed to AI — software development, customer service, and certain kinds of office work — were employed at levels about **19% lower** than you'd expect if they'd kept pace with young people in less-exposed jobs.

**Third: that gap is growing.** A year earlier, it had been 15%.

**Fourth: older, experienced workers in those same jobs were fine.** No comparable gap.

**Fifth: it's happening through hiring, not firing.** People weren't being pushed out. New people just weren't being let in.

**Sixth: wages hadn't moved much.** The change was in *how many* people were employed, not what they were paid.

The researchers were careful. They said plainly that these are patterns, not proof. They can't say for certain that AI caused it. Some of the shift started before AI tools became widespread, and the gap shrinks when you account for things like education.

I respect that caution, and I'm going to hold onto it throughout this book. But I want you to notice *how* the change showed up. Not as a wave of redundancies. As a gap. As jobs that were never advertised.

That's what change looks like before it's obvious.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — A measurable gap has opened for young workers in AI-exposed jobs, through reduced hiring. Economy-wide, there's no collapse. Cause not proven.

---

### Why both things are true at once

You'll hear two stories about AI and jobs, and they sound like they can't both be right.

Story one: *"It's a bloodbath. The entry-level white-collar job is dying."*

Story two: *"Relax. The data shows nothing's happening."*

Both are pointing at real evidence.

The Budget Lab at Yale has been tracking the whole US labour market since ChatGPT was released in late 2022. Their conclusion, updated repeatedly through 2026: the overall mix of jobs in the economy hasn't shifted in any unusual way. Whatever softness there's been in the job market, they say, is "probably not (yet)" AI.

Pay attention to that little word in brackets. *Yet.*

In August 2026, US unemployment sat at 4.1%. That's not a crisis number. Your neighbour who drives a delivery van, your cousin who's a nurse, your mate who does kitchens — their work looks roughly the same as it did three years ago.

But look closer at recent university graduates, and something odd appears. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York tracks graduates aged 22 to 27. Through the first half of 2026, their unemployment rate hovered around 5.6 to 5.7% — *higher* than the national average. Historically, a degree has meant you were *less* likely to be unemployed than everyone else, not more. And about 42% of recent grads were "underemployed," working jobs that don't need their degree.

There are plenty of reasons that could be happening. A cooling economy hits new entrants first. Some degrees don't match what employers want. AI is only one suspect in the room.

But it's in the room.

So here's how I'd put the two stories together:

> **The big picture is stable. The bottom rung of the ladder is wobbling.**

And the bottom rung matters more than people think. It's where you learn. It's where a nineteen-year-old becomes a twenty-nine-year-old who knows what they're doing. If fewer people get on the ladder, you don't feel it this year. You feel it in ten years, when there aren't enough experienced people to go around, and the ones who do exist can name their price.

---

### The companies are starting to say it out loud

For most of 2023 and 2024, companies were careful not to say "we're cutting jobs because of AI." It sounded heartless and it was bad PR.

That changed.

Challenger, Gray & Christmas is an American outplacement firm that has tracked announced job cuts for decades. Every month they record not just how many jobs companies say they're cutting, but *why* the companies say they're cutting them.

In 2026, for five months running, "artificial intelligence" was the number one stated reason. By the end of June, AI had been cited in over 100,000 announced cuts that year — nearly twice as many as in the whole of 2025.

Now, I need to be straight with you about what that number means, because this is where a lot of scary content goes wrong.

Those are *announced reasons*. Companies choose what to say. "We're investing in AI efficiency" sounds a lot better to shareholders than "sales are down and we overhired in 2021." Some of those cuts would have happened anyway, AI or not. The honest reading is: AI is now a reason companies are comfortable giving. Some of the time it's the real reason. Some of the time it's a convenient story.

Either way, it tells you something about where managers' heads are at. In boardrooms, the conversation has shifted from "should we use AI?" to "how many people do we need now that we have it?"

That conversation is happening about your team, whether you're in the room or not.

---

### Why the quiet version is more dangerous

A loud disruption gives you warning. A factory closure is announced months ahead. There's news coverage. There are retraining programs, however inadequate. People rally around each other.

A quiet disruption gives you nothing.

Imagine a thirty-eight-year-old who's been a claims processor at an insurance company for eleven years. She's good at her job. She's never been in trouble. Over three years, four people on her team of twelve retire or move on. None of them are replaced. The work still gets done, because the company brought in software that handles the simple claims automatically. The team is now eight people, and everyone's a bit busier, and there's a new dashboard.

Nobody ever tells her that her job is at risk. It isn't, particularly. Not this year.

But she's now one of eight people doing work that used to take twelve. The next time there's a restructure, the question won't be "should we cut this team?" It'll be "do we need eight, or would five do?" And when she looks around for another claims job — because her manager's a nightmare, or she wants to move closer to her mum — she'll find that the whole industry has been quietly doing the same maths. There are fewer claims jobs everywhere.

That's the pattern this book is trying to get you ahead of. Not a pink slip. A slowly shrinking pond, noticed only when you try to swim somewhere else.

(She's a composite, by the way. I've built her out of the patterns in the data, not from one real person. When I use a real person or a real company in this book, I'll name them and tell you where the information comes from. When I'm painting a picture to help you see something, I'll tell you that too.)

---

### "But it's still mostly hype, isn't it?"

Some of it is. Absolutely.

In 2025, researchers at MIT looked at how companies were getting on with generative AI. They reported that around 95% of corporate AI pilot projects weren't producing any measurable profit. The technology was being bought, trialled, talked about in meetings — and then sitting there not changing the bottom line.

That study got a lot of criticism for how it was done. It was based on a fairly small number of interviews and surveys. But the pattern it described rang true to anyone who's worked in a big organisation: buying the tool is easy; changing how people actually work is hard.

So yes. Plenty of companies are wasting money on AI. Plenty of promises are overblown. If you're a sceptic, you've got good evidence on your side.

But here's the thing I'd ask you to hold in your head alongside that:

**The hype being overblown doesn't mean the change isn't real.**

The internet was wildly overhyped in 1999. Hundreds of companies went bust in 2000 and 2001. People who'd said "this internet thing is a bubble" felt very clever.

They were right about the bubble. They were wrong about the internet.

Within fifteen years it had changed how almost everyone shops, banks, dates, works, finds a plumber and argues with relatives. The hype came first and fell over. The real change came second and stayed.

I think there's a decent chance we're watching the same film again. You don't have to believe the hype to prepare for the change.

---

### What this means for you

If you're young and trying to get your first real job: you're not imagining it. It's harder than it was for people a few years older than you, and part of the reason is that the entry-level tasks you'd have been hired to do are the ones AI is best at. This book is going to show you how to get in by a different door.

If you're mid-career and experienced: the data says you're mostly fine *for now*. Your experience is your armour. But armour works best when you keep it maintained. The people who'll be most secure in five years are experienced workers who also know how to direct AI. That's a very learnable combination.

If you're a parent: the path you took — school, maybe uni, entry-level job, work your way up — might not be the same path available to your kids. Not because it's gone, but because the first few rungs are changing shape. We'll talk about what that means in Part VII.

If you run a business: you're probably already feeling the pull to hire fewer people and use more software. Maybe that's right. But the companies that cut too deep, too fast, have sometimes had to walk it back. We'll look at one famous example in Chapter 7.

If you work with your hands: the next few years look very different for you, and in some ways a lot better. Hold that thought until Chapter 9.

---

### The most important sentence in this chapter

By the time something is obvious to everyone, the cheapest and easiest moment to prepare for it has already passed.

When the news is full of stories about AI unemployment — if it gets there — every course will be full, every career coach will be booked, every "AI skills" job will have five hundred applicants, and the people who started learning two years earlier will already be the ones hiring.

That moment is not now. Now is earlier than that.

You've got more time than you fear. But less than you'd like.

Let's use it.

# Chapter 2
## What AI Actually Is (Without the Jargon)

Let's get something out of the way.

You don't need to understand how an engine works to drive a car. But you do need to know the difference between the brake and the accelerator, roughly how fast you're going, and what that warning light on the dashboard means.

That's the level we're aiming for here. Not engineering. Driving.

---

### The intern who's read everything

The best way I've found to describe a modern AI assistant is this:

> **Imagine the most well-read intern in history. They've read a huge chunk of everything ever written and put online — books, websites, manuals, forums, code, recipes, court cases, arguments. They can write in any style, explain almost anything, and they never get tired or grumpy. But they've never been outside. They've never touched anything, never been responsible for anything, never had to live with the consequences of being wrong.**

That intern is incredibly useful. They'll draft your email in ten seconds. Summarise a forty-page contract. Explain your electricity bill. Suggest six names for your business. Help your kid understand fractions at 9pm when you've forgotten how.

That intern is also, sometimes, confidently and completely wrong. And they'll say the wrong thing in exactly the same calm, polished voice they use when they're right.

Hold both of those in your head, and you understand AI better than most people who talk about it on television.

---

### How it actually works (the two-minute version)

The AI tools most people use — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot and others — are built on something called a **large language model**. Here's what that means in plain English.

Engineers took an enormous amount of text and had a computer program study it over and over, getting better and better at one simple-sounding game: *given everything written so far, what comes next?*

Do that trillions of times, on a big enough computer, and something surprising happens. To get really good at predicting what comes next in a physics textbook, the program has to learn something that looks a lot like physics. To predict what comes next in a legal argument, it has to pick up something that works like legal reasoning. To predict the next line of computer code, it has to learn to code.

Nobody sat down and taught it those things one by one. They emerged from the game.

After that, the companies spend a lot of time training it to be helpful, polite and safe — to answer questions rather than just ramble, to refuse to help with dangerous things, and to admit when it isn't sure (which it still doesn't do nearly enough).

That's it. That's the heart of it.

It's not a person. It's not a search engine that looks things up in a database (although many tools can now search the web as well). It doesn't "know" things the way you know your own phone number. It's more like an extraordinarily practised pattern-finisher — one that has become so good at finishing patterns that, much of the time, it's hard to tell the difference between that and understanding.

Whether there's any real "understanding" in there is a question philosophers and scientists are genuinely arguing about. For your purposes, it doesn't matter much. What matters is what it can do, what it can't, and how often it's wrong.

---

### Why it makes things up

You'll hear the word **"hallucination"** a lot. It means the AI has produced something that sounds right but isn't. A fake quote. A book that doesn't exist. A legal case that never happened. A statistic pulled from thin air.

Why does it do this? Go back to the game. Its deepest instinct is to produce text that *sounds like* what should come next. Most of the time, what sounds right *is* right. But when it doesn't know, it doesn't always go quiet. Sometimes it fills the gap with something that sounds right.

This has already embarrassed real professionals. Lawyers in several countries have been caught submitting court documents that cited cases an AI invented. That's not a hypothetical. Judges have fined people for it.

The newer models do this less than the older ones, and many can now check their answers against web searches. But "less" isn't "never." The golden rule, which you'll see again in Chapter 14:

> **Use AI to think faster. Never use it to stop thinking.**

If the answer matters — money, health, law, your kid's safety, anything you'll put your name to — check it.

---

### The four steps: from chatbot to robot

This is the part that most explanations skip, and it's the part that matters most for your job.

AI isn't one thing. It's a ladder. And it's climbing.

Let me use a kitchen.

**Step 1 — The Recipe Book (a chatbot)**
You ask a question, it gives an answer. "How do I make a white sauce?" It tells you. You do the cooking. This is what most people think AI is, because this is what they tried in 2023.

**Step 2 — The Kitchen Assistant (an AI assistant)**
Now it can look at things you show it and work with your stuff. You take a photo of what's in your fridge and it suggests dinner. You give it your shopping receipts and it works out your weekly food budget. It's working *with* your materials, but you're still doing the doing. It helps; you act.

**Step 3 — The Cook (an AI agent)**
This is the big one. An **agent** is an AI that doesn't just answer — it *does*. You say "sort out dinner for Saturday, six people, one's vegetarian, budget $80," and it finds recipes, builds a shopping list, places the order online, schedules the delivery and sends you a cooking timeline. It takes a goal, breaks it into steps and carries them out, using a computer the way a person would: clicking, typing, searching, filling in forms, checking its own work.

In offices, agents are starting to do things like: go through two hundred invoices, match them against purchase orders, flag the ones that don't add up and draft emails to the suppliers. Or: read a codebase, find a bug, fix it, test the fix and write up what it did.

Agents are where most of the change in the next five years is likely to come from, because they move AI from "helps a person do a task" to "does the task."

**Step 4 — The Cook With Hands (robotics)**
Everything above happens on a screen. The final step is putting that intelligence into a body that can move through the physical world — pick things up, walk through a warehouse, drive a car, fold laundry.

This step is real but much, much further behind. Driverless taxis are carrying hundreds of thousands of paying passengers a week in American cities right now (more on that in Chapter 9). But the humanoid robots you've seen in slick videos are, as of 2026, mostly in trials. According to the 2026 Stanford AI Index, robots succeeded at only around 12% of household tasks they were tested on.

The physical world is hard. Your hands are an astonishing piece of engineering. Keep that in mind — it's one of the most important facts in this book.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Steps 1 and 2 are everyday reality. Step 3 (agents) is in real use and improving fast. Step 4 (robots) is real in narrow areas like driving and some factory work, but general-purpose robots are still early.

---

### It can see, hear and talk now

You'll hear the word **multimodal**. It just means the AI can work with more than text.

The leading systems can now:

- **See** — look at a photo of a rash, a car engine, a spreadsheet, a handwritten note, a building site, and talk about it.
- **Hear** — listen to a meeting and write up the notes, or listen to your voice and respond.
- **Speak** — reply in a natural voice, in real time, with pauses and tone.
- **Make things** — create images, video, music and voices.

That last one is where some of the biggest risks come from. If an AI can produce a realistic voice from a short recording, it can produce *your* voice. Or your daughter's. We'll deal with that properly in Chapter 16, and I promise you'll come out of it with a simple defence that works.

---

### What AI is *not*

A few myths worth clearing up, because they get in the way of good decisions:

**It's not a person.** It can sound caring, and it can be genuinely helpful when you're struggling. But it doesn't love you, miss you or worry about you when you close the app. That matters a lot when we talk about AI companions in Chapter 17.

**It's not all-knowing.** It has blind spots, gaps and biases picked up from whatever it learned from. It can be out of date. It can be wrong about local laws, local prices and local people.

**It's not magic, and it's not nothing.** The two most dangerous attitudes are "it can do anything" and "it's just autocomplete." Both lead to bad decisions. The truth is somewhere uncomfortable in between: it's astonishingly capable at some things and surprisingly useless at others, and the line between the two keeps moving.

**It's not one company's product.** Several big companies (and many smaller ones, and several countries) are competing hard. According to the 2026 Stanford AI Index, the top American and Chinese models were within a few percentage points of each other on key tests as of early 2026. That competition is one reason things are moving so fast.

---

### Why this matters for you

You don't need to know how a transformer model works. (That's the technical name for the design most of these systems use. You can forget it now.)

What you need is a working sense of what this intern can do, so you can answer three questions about your own life:

1. **Which parts of my work could the intern do?**
2. **Which parts could the intern help me do faster?**
3. **Which parts need someone who's been outside, touched things and can be held responsible?**

That third category is going to become the most important thing you own. We'll build it out properly in Chapter 6.

---

> ### DO THIS NOW
>
> **1. Have your first *real* conversation with an AI.** Not "tell me a joke." Try this: *"I work as a [your job]. Here's what a normal day looks like for me: [describe it in 4–5 sentences]. Which parts of my day could you help with, and which parts couldn't you help with at all? Be honest."* (15 minutes)
>
> **2. Catch it being wrong.** Ask it something you know a lot about — your hometown, your trade, your favourite sport, your medical condition. Push it until you find a mistake. You will. Remember how confidently it said it. (10 minutes)
>
> **3. Show it a photo.** Take a picture of something from your day — a form, a broken tap, a label, a page of your kid's homework — and ask it to explain what it sees. (5 minutes)

# Chapter 3
## The Doubling Clock

There's an old riddle about a pond.

A single lily pad appears on a pond. Every day, the number of lily pads doubles. After thirty days, the pond is completely covered.

On what day was the pond half covered?

Most people's gut says day fifteen. The answer is day twenty-nine.

And here's the part that matters: on day twenty-five, the pond was only about 3% covered. If you'd walked past that pond on day twenty-five, you'd have seen a few lily pads in the corner and thought, *nothing much going on here.*

Four days later, it's gone.

Our brains aren't built for doubling. We think in straight lines. Add a bit each year. The world mostly works that way, so our instincts do too. But some things don't add. They multiply. And when something multiplies, the early part looks boring, right up until it doesn't.

This chapter is about whether AI is one of those things.

---

### A stopwatch for machines

For a long time, the way people measured AI progress was with tests. Can it pass the bar exam? Can it solve maths olympiad problems? Can it score well on a medical licensing test?

Those tests matter, but they have a problem. They measure whether AI can *answer questions*, not whether it can *do work*. And they get "used up": once AI aces a test, the test stops telling you anything.

So in 2025, a small independent research group in California called METR came up with a different measuring stick. I think it's the single most useful number in this whole debate, so let me explain it carefully.

They took a large set of real tasks — mostly software and technical work, the kind of thing that people get paid to do. Then they timed skilled humans doing them. Some tasks took a person two minutes. Some took an hour. Some took a full working day or more.

Then they gave the same tasks to AI systems and asked: **how long a task, measured in human time, can the AI complete successfully about half the time?**

That's the "time horizon." Think of it as the size of a job you could hand to the AI and walk away from, with a coin-flip chance it's done when you come back.

Here's what they found when they lined up AI systems from 2019 onwards:

- In 2019, the best systems could reliably handle tasks that took a human a few *seconds*.
- By early 2023, a few *minutes*.
- By 2025, around an *hour* or so.
- By early 2026, METR's own estimates put the best models at *several hours* of human-expert work — though with very wide margins of error.

When they plotted that on a graph, it made a remarkably steady line. Over the whole period from 2019, the length of task AI could handle **doubled roughly every seven months.** In METR's January 2026 update, the doubling since 2024 looked even faster — closer to every three to four months.

By mid-2026, METR was saying something striking: their test was running out of road. The newest models were scoring near the top of what their task set could reliably measure — around sixteen hours of human work.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — The length of task AI can complete on its own (at ~50% success) has been doubling roughly every 4–7 months for several years, according to the leading independent measurement.

---

### What doubling feels like

Let's make that concrete.

Say an AI can do a one-hour task today. If the doubling continues at seven months:

- In 7 months: 2-hour tasks
- In 14 months: 4-hour tasks — half a working day
- In 21 months: 8-hour tasks — a full day
- In 28 months: 2-day tasks
- In 35 months: nearly a working week

Just under three years from "an hour" to "most of a week."

At the faster four-month pace, it's quicker than that.

Now, I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the kind of calculation that gets breathlessly shared online with "AND BY 2030 IT WILL DO A YEAR OF WORK IN A SECOND!!!" in capital letters.

Trends don't have to continue. Plenty of things that doubled for a while have stopped. The lily pond riddle has a finite pond.

So let's look hard at the caveats. Because they're real.

---

### The honest small print

**1. "Half the time" isn't "reliably."**
That time horizon is the length of task the AI completes successfully *about 50% of the time*. Would you hire someone who got the job done half the time? For a lot of real work — accounts, medicine, anything legal — you need it right almost every time. When METR measured at 80% success instead of 50%, the time horizons were much shorter. A system that can sometimes do a sixteen-hour task might only reliably do a three-hour one.

**2. It's mostly technical work.**
METR's tasks are mostly software and research-type tasks. AI tends to be strongest at exactly that kind of thing. It doesn't automatically follow that the same curve applies to managing a building site, calming an upset customer, or teaching a room of fourteen-year-olds.

**3. The margins are huge.**
METR themselves say their confidence intervals are "very wide." For one top model in early 2026, their best estimate was around five hours, but the plausible range stretched from under three hours to over twelve. They also noted that only a handful of their longest tasks had actually been timed with real humans. They're being honest about the limits of their ruler. I respect that.

**4. A test isn't a workplace.**
Doing a well-defined task in a clean test environment is different from doing it inside a real company with messy data, unclear instructions, office politics and a boss who changes their mind.

So the fair summary is:

> **Trust the trend. Distrust any single number.**

The exact figures are shaky. The direction is not. Every independent measurement I've seen points the same way: these systems are getting able to do longer, more complicated chunks of work on their own, and they're doing it fast.

The International AI Safety Report — a major review published in February 2026, led by the Turing Award-winning scientist Yoshua Bengio and written by over a hundred experts from more than thirty countries — reached a similar conclusion. Capabilities are advancing quickly, and faster than the rules meant to manage them.

---

### The Jagged Edge

Here's the second big idea in this chapter, and it's the one that will save you from embarrassing mistakes.

AI is not evenly good.

In 2023, researchers from Harvard Business School and elsewhere ran a proper experiment with 758 consultants from Boston Consulting Group — some of the most highly trained professionals in business. Half got access to GPT-4, one of the leading AI models of the time. Half didn't. Then they gave everyone realistic consulting tasks.

On tasks that the AI was good at, the consultants using it:

- completed about **12% more tasks**,
- finished about **25% faster**,
- and produced work rated roughly **40% higher in quality**.

That's a huge jump. For elite professionals.

But the researchers had slipped in a trick. One task was chosen specifically because it *looked* like something the AI would be good at, but actually needed careful judgement that the AI tended to get wrong.

On that task, consultants using AI were **19 percentage points less likely** to get the right answer than the ones working alone.

They trusted the machine. The machine was confidently wrong. And being smart and highly trained didn't save them.

The researchers called this the **"jagged technological frontier."** I call it the Jagged Edge. Picture a coastline instead of a straight wall. In some places, the AI's abilities stretch way out past what you'd expect. In others, right next door, they fall well short. And from the inside, you can't see where the coastline goes. The AI sounds exactly as confident on both sides of it.

> **The danger isn't that AI is stupid. The danger is that it's brilliant and stupid at the same time, and it doesn't tell you which one you're getting.**

This is exactly why the people who'll do best aren't the ones who use AI the most. They're the ones who know *where the edge is* in their own field — and check the work that falls near it.

That knowledge comes from experience. Which is one more reason experienced workers have an advantage, if they learn to use it.

---

### The perception trap

One more study, because it's one of my favourites and it's slightly uncomfortable.

In early 2025, METR ran another experiment. They took sixteen experienced software developers working on big, real, open-source projects they already knew inside out. For each task, a coin flip decided whether the developer could use AI tools or not.

Before starting, the developers predicted AI would make them about 24% faster.

Afterwards, they *believed* it had made them about 20% faster.

It had actually made them about **19% slower.**

METR has since said this result is "historical" — the tools have improved a lot since early 2025, and they've changed how they run these studies. So I'm not telling you AI slows people down. In many other studies, it clearly speeds them up.

I'm telling you something about *us*.

We are bad at judging whether AI is actually helping. It *feels* productive. The words appear so fast. It's so fluent. That feeling is not the same as the work being better or quicker.

So measure. Time yourself with and without. Check the output. Don't just trust the buzz.

---

### Where this leaves us

Let me pull the chapter together into something you can carry around.

**One:** The amount of work AI can do on its own is growing fast, and has been for years. The best measurement we have suggests it's doubling every several months.

**Two:** The exact numbers are uncertain, and the tests don't cover everything. But the direction is consistent across many different measurements.

**Three:** AI is jagged. Great at some things, bad at others, and it sounds equally sure of itself either way.

**Four:** Our own sense of whether it's helping is unreliable. Measure, don't assume.

And here's the thing that ties it back to the pond.

If you look at AI today and think "it's useful, but it can't do my job" — you might be completely right. *Today.* The question to ask isn't "can it do my job now?" It's: **"If the length of work it can handle keeps doubling a few more times, which parts of my job does it reach, and when?"**

That's not a scary question. It's a planning question. And planning questions have answers.

Part II is where we start answering it.

# Chapter 4
## 2027–2028: The Agent Years

I'm going to do something in this part of the book that most writers on this subject avoid.

I'm going to describe the future year by year, as specifically as I honestly can. And every time I say something, I'm going to tell you how confident I am.

I'm doing it this way because vague warnings are useless. "AI will transform everything" doesn't tell you whether to renew your lease, change your course, have a conversation with your boss or start a side business. Specifics do. Even specifics that might be wrong — as long as you know how likely they are to be wrong — are more useful than fog.

So here's my best, labelled attempt at 2027 and 2028.

---

### The big shift: from "help me" to "do it"

If 2023 to 2025 were the chatbot years — when most people's experience of AI was typing a question and getting an answer — I expect 2027 and 2028 to be remembered as the **agent years**.

Remember the kitchen from Chapter 2? This is when AI moves, for a lot of office work, from Recipe Book to Cook.

What does that look like in practice? Something like this.

It's a Tuesday in 2028. A small accounting practice with six staff has an AI agent set up on its systems. Overnight, the agent has:

- downloaded bank feeds for forty clients,
- matched about nine in ten transactions automatically,
- flagged the rest with a note explaining what's odd about each,
- drafted reminder emails to the six clients whose paperwork is late,
- and prepared first drafts of three quarterly tax returns.

When the team comes in, their day isn't *doing* that work. It's *checking*, *fixing*, *deciding* and *talking to clients* about it.

That's not science fiction. Pieces of it already exist in 2026. What changes is that it becomes normal, reliable enough to trust with real work, and cheap enough for a six-person firm.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — By 2028, AI agents that complete multi-hour chunks of computer-based work (with human checking) will be common in offices, especially in finance, admin, customer service, software, marketing and research. How reliable they are will vary a lot by task.

Why do I rate that as likely rather than possible? Three reasons. The Doubling Clock from Chapter 3 is already there on technical tasks. The big technology companies are building exactly this and selling it to businesses right now. And the business incentive is enormous: work that runs overnight, doesn't take sick days and costs a fraction of a salary.

Why not higher than "likely"? Because, as we saw in Chapter 1, most companies are slow and clumsy at actually changing how they work. The technology will be ready before a lot of workplaces are.

---

### What this means for jobs in 2027–2028

**The entry-level squeeze gets tighter.**
The Stanford payroll research showed young workers in AI-exposed jobs falling behind through 2025 and 2026, through reduced hiring. If agents get better at exactly the tasks junior staff used to do — first drafts, data cleaning, basic research, simple code, routine customer queries — I'd expect that gap to keep widening in 2027 and 2028.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — Entry-level hiring in AI-exposed office roles stays weak or gets weaker.

**Teams get smaller, not empty.**
The more common pattern won't be "the whole department's gone." It'll be "the department used to be fifteen people and it's now nine, and the nine are expected to use AI to do what fifteen did." Headcount shrinks through retirements, resignations and roles that aren't refilled.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — Gradual shrinking of some team sizes, mainly through attrition rather than mass layoffs.

**Some big, public layoffs happen — and some of them get walked back.**
Some companies will cut hard and announce it proudly. Some of those will discover, like the Swedish finance company Klarna did (Chapter 7), that customers still want a human when things get complicated, and quality drops when you cut too deep.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY**

**Experienced people who can direct AI become worth more.**
Someone with ten years in a field who can manage three agents and check their work is suddenly doing the output of a small team. Employers will pay for that. The Stanford data already shows experienced workers in exposed jobs holding up much better than young ones.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY**

**Most jobs still exist and look broadly familiar.**
This is important and it gets forgotten. In 2028, nurses still nurse. Electricians still wire houses. Teachers still teach. Chefs still cook. Hairdressers still cut hair. Most jobs change *around the edges* — the admin, the paperwork, the scheduling — while the core stays human.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY**

---

### Industries most likely to feel it first

Based on where the evidence is pointing, here's roughly the order I'd expect people to notice change in, over 2027–2028.

**1. Software development** — already changing. AI writes a lot of routine code. Stanford's AI Index found employment of software developers aged 22–25 had fallen nearly 20% since 2024. Senior developers who can design systems, review AI code and understand the business stay in demand.

**2. Customer service and call centres** — AI handles more of the first line. Humans handle the angry, the complicated, the vulnerable and the high-value.

**3. Admin, bookkeeping, data entry, claims, back-office processing** — lots of rule-based computer work that agents are well suited to.

**4. Marketing, content and copywriting** — volume content becomes cheap. Strategy, taste, brand voice and real relationships hold value.

**5. Paralegal, research and junior analyst work** — document review, first-draft research, summarising, comparing.

**6. Translation and transcription** — already heavily affected.

What these share: the work happens on a computer, it's mostly about processing information, and the quality can be checked afterwards.

---

### What *won't* change much by 2028

Honestly? A lot.

- **Hands-on trades** — plumbing, electrical, carpentry, HVAC, roofing. AI helps with quotes and paperwork. It isn't crawling under your house.
- **Care work** — aged care, disability support, childcare, nursing. The human, physical, emotional core stays.
- **Hospitality** — cooking, serving, bartending.
- **Emergency services, policing, defence.**
- **Most roles with legal liability and a licence** — the doctor, the pharmacist, the engineer who signs off the building. AI assists; a human is still accountable.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — Physical, in-person, care-based and licensed work remains mostly human through 2028, with AI changing the admin around it.

Robots are coming for some of this eventually. But in 2027–2028, the evidence says they're still largely in trials for general-purpose work, and driverless vehicles are expanding city by city rather than everywhere at once. Chapter 9 goes into detail.

---

### The dark side of the agent years

I won't pretend this period is only about jobs and productivity.

**Scams go industrial.**
Every tool that helps a small business write a thousand personalised emails helps a scammer write a thousand personalised scams. In 2025, the FBI recorded Americans reporting nearly $21 billion in losses to online crime, with people over sixty reporting $7.75 billion — up 59% in a single year. For the first time, the FBI's annual report included a section specifically on AI-related complaints.

AI voices and videos get cheaper and better. I'd expect voice-clone scams targeting families — "Mum, I've been in an accident, I need money" — to become far more common in 2027–2028.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — AI-enabled fraud grows substantially. Chapter 16 gives you the defence.

**Trust in what you see online drops further.**
When anyone can generate a realistic video of anyone saying anything, "I saw it with my own eyes" stops meaning much online. That has big consequences for news, elections and family arguments.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY**

**The first proper rules arrive — slowly.**
In Europe, the EU AI Act's transparency rules — telling people when they're talking to an AI, labelling AI-generated content — started applying in August 2026. The stricter rules for "high-risk" AI (in hiring, lending, education and similar areas) were pushed back, and now apply from December 2027 for most systems. In the US, it's a patchwork of state laws, like California's rules on AI companion chatbots for minors, which took effect in January 2026.

My honest view: rules will help at the edges. They won't stop the underlying change, and they'll mostly arrive after the problems they're responding to.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW / LIKELY** — Regulation is real but slow, patchy and reactive.

---

### Skills that get more valuable in 2027–2028

If I had to bet on what employers will pay more for in this window, it'd be these:

1. **Directing AI** — breaking a job into pieces an agent can do, briefing it well, checking the output. (Chapter 22.)
2. **Judgement in your field** — knowing where the Jagged Edge is. What looks right but isn't.
3. **Client and customer relationships** — trust, calm, reading people.
4. **Making AI actually work inside a business** — setting it up, fitting it into real processes, training people. Remember that study where most corporate AI pilots weren't producing results? Someone needs to fix that.
5. **Cybersecurity and fraud awareness.**
6. **Physical and technical trades**, especially electrical and anything connected to power, cooling and construction for the data centre boom.
7. **Being the human in the loop** — the person who can be held responsible.

---

### What to do *before* 2027–2028 arrives

This is the whole point of the chapter. Here's what I'd do in the next twelve months, knowing what's likely coming:

**If you're in an office job:** Get genuinely good at using AI for your own work this year, while it's still an advantage rather than an expectation. Volunteer to be the person on your team who figures it out. Being the one who *brings* the change is a very different position from being the one it *happens to*.

**If you're early in your career:** Don't rely on the traditional entry-level route alone. Build proof of what you can do — a portfolio, real projects, freelance work, anything someone can see. (Chapter 11 — "Proof Over Paper.")

**If you're a manager or business owner:** Map which tasks in your business agents could do in 2028. Decide now how you'll handle it humanely — retraining, redeployment, honest communication. People remember how they were treated.

**If you work with your hands:** Use AI for the admin you hate — quotes, invoices, scheduling, marketing. It's the biggest near-term win available to you.

**If you're a parent:** Have the scam conversation with your family now (Chapter 16). Set up your safe word this week.

**Everyone:** Start building your cash cushion (Chapter 12). Uncertain times reward people with a buffer.

---

### The feeling to watch out for

There's a trap in 2027–2028 that I want to warn you about.

Because most jobs will still look familiar — because the nurse is still nursing and your office still has people in it — it will be very easy to look around and conclude that it was all hype.

Some of it will have been. But remember the pond. The agent years are likely to be the part of the curve where the lily pads are clearly spreading but most of the water is still open.

That's not the time to relax. It's the best time you'll ever get to prepare.

# Chapter 5
## 2029–2031: Three Futures

Here's where honest writers have to change gear.

For 2027 and 2028, we can see the trend lines, the products being built and the money being spent. We can make reasonable calls.

By 2029 to 2031, the fog thickens. Small differences now — how fast the Doubling Clock keeps ticking, how quickly businesses actually adopt this stuff, what governments do, whether there's a recession, whether there's some unexpected breakthrough or some unexpected wall — compound into very different worlds.

So instead of pretending to know which world we'll get, I'm going to give you three. Think of them like a weather forecaster saying "30% chance of storms, 50% chance of showers, 20% chance of sunshine." You pack for all three, and you watch the sky.

For each future, I'll tell you what daily life might feel like, who gets hit, where the opportunities are, and — most importantly — **the warning signs that would tell you it's the one we're heading into.**

Then I'll give you the moves that work no matter which one shows up.

---

## Future One: The Slow Burn

### What happens
AI keeps improving, but the jumps get smaller. Maybe the Doubling Clock slows down: the systems keep getting better at tests, but turning that into reliable real-world work proves harder than expected. Or maybe the technology races ahead but businesses, as they often do, adopt it slowly — held back by messy data, legal worries, cost, habit and plain old organisational inertia.

Think of how long it took for businesses to properly use computers. The technology was around for decades before most offices were transformed.

### What life feels like in 2031
Most people use AI every day, the way we use search engines — for writing, research, planning, homework help, customer service. It's useful and unremarkable. Some jobs have shrunk: there are fewer junior admin roles, fewer call centre staff, fewer entry-level coders. But new roles have appeared too, and the overall job market looks broadly normal, like previous waves of technology.

### Who gets hit
Mainly the young and the entry-level in exposed office jobs — the pattern we're already seeing, continuing at a similar pace. Some industries (translation, basic copywriting, some call centre work) shrink significantly.

### Opportunities
Lots of work helping slow-moving businesses finally get AI working properly. Big advantages for early adopters in every field, because the gap between people who use AI well and people who don't keeps widening.

### Warning signs this is our future
- Unemployment stays roughly flat through 2028.
- Headlines about AI failures and disappointing results outnumber breakthrough stories.
- The Stanford-style young-worker gap stops widening.
- AI company revenues grow more slowly than their spending.

> **My rough odds: one of the more likely outcomes, but less likely than many sceptics assume.**

---

## Future Two: The Steady Climb

### What happens
The trend lines roughly continue. The Doubling Clock keeps ticking, maybe a bit slower, maybe not. By 2030, AI agents can reliably handle a large share of computer-based tasks that take a person a day or more. Businesses adopt it unevenly — fast in some sectors, slow in others — but steadily.

Robots begin moving from trials into real paid work in warehouses, factories and logistics, although general-purpose home robots are still mostly a novelty.

### What life feels like in 2031
This is a big reshuffle, not a collapse. Lots of people are doing different jobs from the ones they did in 2025. Many office jobs have been redesigned around AI, with fewer people each doing more. The "job" of lots of professionals has shifted from *doing the work* to *directing, checking and owning the work*.

Unemployment may rise somewhat, especially for certain groups, and some regions and industries are hit hard. There's political pressure for retraining schemes, stronger unemployment support and new rules. There's also a lot of new work: in AI implementation, in trades powering the boom, in care, in anything where a trusted human is the product.

The IMF has warned that in most scenarios, AI is likely to worsen inequality unless governments act. In this future, that shows. People who learned to direct AI early and who own assets do very well. People who didn't adapt fall further behind.

### Who gets hit
Entry-level and mid-level office workers in exposed roles, especially people whose work was mostly processing information. Some professionals whose value was mainly knowing things rather than judging, deciding or relating. Drivers in cities where autonomous vehicles expand.

### Opportunities
Very large. One person with the right skills and AI agents can run a business that used to need ten staff. Trades connected to power, construction and maintenance are in high demand. Human-premium services — the ones people want *from a person* — grow. Anyone who can help businesses and families through the transition has plenty of work.

### Warning signs this is our future
- METR-style measurements show AI completing multi-day tasks reliably by 2028.
- The young-worker gap keeps widening *and* starts appearing for mid-career workers.
- Big companies publicly report smaller workforces alongside growing profits.
- Governments start seriously debating income support linked to automation.
- Robot deployments move from hundreds to many thousands.

> **My rough odds: I think this is the most likely of the three, but I hold that loosely.**

---

## Future Three: The Fast Break

### What happens
Something speeds up. Perhaps AI systems become good enough at AI research itself to help build their own successors much faster. Perhaps there's a breakthrough in reliability, so that agents go from "right half the time" to "right almost every time" on long tasks. Perhaps the heads of some AI labs, who have said they expect extremely capable AI before the end of this decade, turn out to be right.

In May 2025, Dario Amodei — the head of Anthropic, one of the leading AI companies — warned that AI could eliminate up to half of entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years, pushing unemployment as high as 10–20%. That's a serious person, with inside knowledge, making a serious warning. It's also one person's warning, not a consensus. (By 2026, he was also emphasising the other side: that as AI makes work cheaper, demand for it may grow.)

### What life feels like in 2031
Disruption is fast and widespread. Many kinds of office and knowledge work are substantially automated in a few years. Unemployment rises sharply for white-collar workers — a shock that, unlike most previous waves of automation, hits the educated middle class hard. Governments scramble. Emergency policies appear. Some things get much cheaper. Enormous wealth concentrates among those who own the AI systems.

At the same time, science and medicine might accelerate dramatically. Some very good things could happen alongside some very disruptive ones.

### Who gets hit
Broadly, anyone whose work is done mostly at a computer and mostly involves processing information, including many professionals who currently feel safe.

### Opportunities
Hard to predict precisely. Physical, human, local and trusted work becomes relatively more valuable. So does owning assets and businesses rather than only selling your time. And people who are skilled at directing AI systems would be in a very strong position.

### Warning signs this is our future
- AI systems start reliably doing week-long or month-long projects.
- AI labs announce major breakthroughs that independent researchers confirm.
- Graduate unemployment jumps sharply in a single year.
- Major companies announce very large cuts that *stick*, without walking them back.
- Governments start emergency job programs or income support specifically tied to AI.

> **My rough odds: less likely than the other two, but not a fringe idea. Several of the people closest to the technology take it seriously. That's reason enough to prepare for it, without planning your whole life around it.**

---

### The Wild Cards

A few things that could happen in any of the three futures, that I can't predict and won't pretend to:

- **A serious AI accident or misuse** — a major cyber-attack, a financial market event, or an AI system behaving in a seriously harmful way — that triggers a big public and political backlash.
- **A major recession** unrelated to AI, which would hit jobs hard and tangle up the picture.
- **An energy or chip bottleneck** — AI needs enormous amounts of electricity (the International Energy Agency expects data centre electricity use to roughly double by 2030) and advanced chips from a small number of factories. Disruption to either could slow everything down.
- **Geopolitical conflict** involving the countries and companies that make AI chips.
- **General-purpose home robots arriving much sooner than expected.** Possible. I wouldn't bet on it in this window.

> **FORECAST LABEL: WILD CARD** — any of these could reshape the timeline. None can be reliably predicted.

---

### The No-Regret Moves

So how do you plan when you don't know which future you're in?

You do the things that pay off in *all three*. Economists sometimes call these "no-regret moves." Here they are:

**1. Build a cash cushion.**
In the Slow Burn, it's just good sense. In the Steady Climb, it buys you time to retrain. In the Fast Break, it could be the difference between a stressful year and losing your home. (Chapter 12.)

**2. Become genuinely good at directing AI.**
In the Slow Burn, it makes you better than your peers. In the Steady Climb, it's what employers pay for. In the Fast Break, it's one of the few skills that goes *up* in value. (Chapters 14, 15, 22.)

**3. Strengthen your human moats.**
Relationships, trust, hands-on skills, judgement, accountability. In every future, these hold value longer than pure information work. (Chapter 6.)

**4. Build a second income stream.**
Even a small one. Two legs of a table wobble. Three stand. (Chapter 12–13.)

**5. Protect your family from scams.**
AI-enabled fraud is rising in every future. (Chapter 16.)

**6. Teach your kids to think, not just to answer.**
The skills that matter in every future — thinking clearly, learning fast, working with people, building things. (Chapter 19.)

**7. Stay informed, calmly.**
Check the warning signs once a quarter. Not daily. Not doom-scrolling. A calm quarterly check-in with the list above.

None of these require you to know which future is coming. All of them make you stronger in any of them.

---

### Why three futures instead of one

I know it would be more satisfying if I just told you what's going to happen.

Plenty of people will. Some will say it's all hype and nothing will change. Others will say we're all doomed by 2029. Both groups will sell a lot of content.

But the reason I'm giving you three futures is that **the right way to prepare for uncertainty is not to guess the answer. It's to become someone who does well across the range of answers.**

That's what the rest of this book is for.

# Chapter 6
## The Task Test

"Will AI take my job?"

It's the question everyone asks. And I'm going to tell you something that might sound annoying at first: it's the wrong question.

Not because it doesn't matter. Because it can't be answered. A job isn't one thing. It's a bundle of dozens of different tasks, tied together with a job title and a pay slip.

A receptionist doesn't "do reception." A receptionist answers phones, books appointments, greets people, calms down upset customers, handles deliveries, notices when the regular client looks unwell, reminds the boss about the meeting they've forgotten, knows which supplier to ring when the printer breaks, and a hundred other things.

AI doesn't come for jobs. It comes for **tasks**. One at a time. Some fall quickly. Some fall slowly. Some don't fall at all.

So the useful question isn't "will AI take my job?" It's:

> **"Which of my tasks can AI do, which can it help me do, and which need a human — and what happens to my job when that mix changes?"**

That question *can* be answered. And by the end of this chapter, you'll have answered it for yourself, on paper, in about twenty minutes.

---

### The six ways AI can change a job

Before we test your job, it helps to know what we're testing for. When AI touches a job, it can do six quite different things. People lump them together, and that's where a lot of confusion comes from.

**1. AI replaces a task.**
One thing you used to do, you don't do anymore. A bookkeeper used to type in receipts; now the software reads them. The job still exists; one task is gone.

**2. AI changes the job.**
Enough tasks shift that the job looks different. A marketing coordinator who used to spend most of their week writing copy now spends most of it choosing, editing and testing AI-written copy — and talking to customers to work out what to say.

**3. AI reduces the number of people needed.**
Each person can do more, so the company needs fewer of them. Nobody's job disappears exactly — there are just fewer of those jobs. This is the "hiring freeze nobody announced" from Chapter 1.

**4. AI creates new demand.**
This is the one people forget. When something gets cheaper, people often want more of it. When spreadsheets arrived, bookkeepers worried. What actually happened was an explosion in financial analysis, because suddenly you could run numbers that were impractical before. If AI makes legal help cheaper, perhaps many more people get legal help. If it makes software cheaper, perhaps every small business gets custom software. That can create *more* work, not less.

**5. Robots replace physical tasks.**
A different track with a different timeline. Driving, warehouse picking, some factory work. Slower and more expensive than software, but real.

**6. New jobs appear.**
Jobs that didn't exist before: people who build and manage AI agents, people who check AI output in regulated industries, people who help businesses set it all up, people who design how humans and AI work together. We can't list them all yet, the same way nobody in 1995 could have listed "social media manager."

When you look at your own job, all six might be happening at once. Some tasks disappearing. The job changing shape. Fewer people doing it. More demand in some areas. New roles popping up next door.

That's why we test tasks, not jobs.

---

### The A-A-A Test

Every task you do falls into one of three buckets.

**AUTOMATE** — AI can do this task, now or within a couple of years, with little human input. A human might glance at the result, but the work itself is done by the machine.

*Examples: typing up meeting notes; basic data entry; first-draft product descriptions; sorting emails; simple scheduling; translating a standard document; routine code.*

**ASSIST** — AI can make you faster or better at this task, but a human still needs to lead it, shape it, check it or finish it.

*Examples: writing a report; preparing a quote; researching a problem; analysing figures; planning a lesson; drafting a legal letter; diagnosing a fault from a customer's description.*

**ANCHOR** — This task needs a human, and is likely to for years. It's anchored in the physical world, in human relationships, in trust or in responsibility.

*Examples: fixing a leaking pipe; comforting a scared patient; negotiating with an angry client; deciding whether to hire someone; signing off a building as safe; teaching a nervous kid to swim; being the person who says "I'll take responsibility for this."*

---

### The Five Moats

How do you know if a task is an Anchor? Look for the Five Moats. A moat is the water around a castle — the thing that makes it hard to get in. These are the five things that make a task hard for AI to take.

**1. HANDS — It happens in the physical world.**
Does the task need someone to be physically present, to touch, lift, fix, build, clean, carry or move things in unpredictable places? The physical world is hard for machines, especially outside controlled environments like factories. Your kitchen, a building site, an old house with dodgy wiring — these are still very difficult for robots.

**2. HEART — It depends on human connection.**
Does the task depend on people *wanting* to deal with another human? Comfort, care, motivation, persuasion, trust built over years. A grieving family doesn't want a chatbot. A nervous first-time home buyer wants a person they trust.

**3. TRUST — People need to know who's behind it.**
Does the task depend on a real, known person or relationship? Your reputation in your local area. The client who's been with you for twelve years. The customer who comes back because they like *you*.

**4. TASTE — It needs judgement about what's good.**
Does the task require knowing what's right, what fits, what works for *this* situation, *this* client, *this* brand? AI can produce a thousand options. Someone still has to know which one is right and why. That comes from experience.

**5. ACCOUNTABILITY — Someone has to be responsible.**
Does the task require someone who can be held responsible if it goes wrong? A licence, a signature, a legal duty, a professional reputation on the line. Society doesn't let software sign off on a bridge, prescribe medication without oversight or take the blame in court. That's likely to remain true for some time — and in some areas, it may become *more* true, as regulations require a human in the loop.

A task with **none** of these moats is likely to be automated sooner or later.

A task with **one** moat is safer, but that moat might get crossed.

A task with **two or more** moats is your strongest ground. Build your future there.

---

### The Worksheet

Get a piece of paper, or open a note on your phone. This takes about twenty minutes, and it's the most useful twenty minutes in this book.

**Step 1: List your tasks.**
Write down fifteen things you actually do in your job. Be specific. Not "customer service" — "answer customer emails about late orders." Not "admin" — "update the job tracking spreadsheet."

If you're stuck, think through a typical week, day by day. What did you do on Monday morning? Tuesday afternoon?

**Step 2: Estimate the time.**
Next to each task, write roughly what percentage of your working week it takes. They should add up to about 100%. It doesn't have to be exact.

**Step 3: Sort them.**
Mark each task **AU** (Automate), **AS** (Assist) or **AN** (Anchor).

If you're not sure, ask yourself:
- Could a smart intern who'd read everything do this from a computer, with clear instructions? → probably **AU**
- Could that intern do a decent first draft, but I'd have to fix it or finish it? → probably **AS**
- Does it need my hands, my relationships, my judgement or my signature? → probably **AN**

**Step 4: Count the moats.**
For each Anchor task, write which of the five moats protect it: Hands, Heart, Trust, Taste, Accountability.

**Step 5: Work out your Exposure Score.**
Add up the time percentages:
- Total % of time on AU tasks = ___
- Total % of time on AS tasks = ___
- Total % of time on AN tasks = ___

Here's how to read it:

| If your Automate % is… | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Under 20%** | **Low exposure.** Your job is mostly protected by moats. AI will change your admin, not your core. Focus on using AI to clear the boring stuff and grow your Anchor work. |
| **20–40%** | **Moderate exposure.** Your job will noticeably change. You'll likely be expected to do more in the same time. Get good at AI now, and shift your time towards Assist and Anchor work. |
| **40–60%** | **High exposure.** A significant chunk of what you're paid for could be done by AI in the next few years. Your job may change a lot, or there may be fewer of them. Start actively building new skills this year. |
| **Over 60%** | **Very high exposure.** Your role, as currently designed, is at serious risk of shrinking or disappearing. This isn't a verdict on *you* — it's a verdict on the task mix. Your experience is valuable; it needs a new home. Chapter 11 is your next stop. |

**Step 6: The Future Job Description.**
Now write down what your job would look like if all the AU tasks disappeared and the AS tasks took half the time. What's left? What would you fill the freed-up time with?

That's the job you want to be growing into. Because that's the job that will still exist.

---

### Worked Example 1: Medical Receptionist

Let's run the test on a medical receptionist at a busy GP practice.

| Task | Time % | A-A-A | Moats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answering phones and booking appointments | 25% | AU | — |
| Checking in patients at the desk | 10% | AS | Heart |
| Processing payments and billing queries | 10% | AU | — |
| Updating patient records and forms | 10% | AU | — |
| Handling repeat prescription requests | 5% | AS | Accountability |
| Calming upset or distressed patients | 10% | AN | Heart, Trust |
| Noticing when someone seems very unwell and alerting a nurse | 5% | AN | Heart, Accountability |
| Managing the waiting room | 5% | AN | Hands, Heart |
| Chasing test results and referrals | 10% | AS | — |
| Coordinating doctors' schedules and sorting clashes | 5% | AS | Taste |
| General admin, filing, scanning, emails | 5% | AU | — |

**Automate:** 25 + 10 + 10 + 5 = **50%**
**Assist:** 10 + 5 + 10 + 5 = **30%**
**Anchor:** 10 + 5 + 5 = **20%**

That's **high exposure**. AI phone systems that can book appointments already exist. Billing and records are going the same way.

But look at what's left. The human face of the practice. The person who notices the elderly man looks grey and gets a nurse. The one who calms the mother whose child has a fever. The one who knows the regulars.

A smart receptionist in this position could grow into a **patient experience coordinator** — someone who handles the human side, manages the AI systems that do the booking, deals with anything the systems can't, and makes sure vulnerable patients don't fall through the cracks. That job likely pays more and is more secure. But it only goes to the person who can operate the new systems *and* is great with people.

---

### Worked Example 2: Accountant at a Small Firm

| Task | Time % | A-A-A | Moats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data entry and bank reconciliation | 20% | AU | — |
| Preparing standard tax returns | 20% | AS→AU | Accountability |
| Preparing financial statements | 10% | AS | Accountability |
| Answering routine client emails | 10% | AS | — |
| Advising clients on business decisions | 10% | AN | Trust, Taste, Accountability |
| Tax planning for complex situations | 10% | AS | Taste, Accountability |
| Meeting clients, building relationships | 10% | AN | Heart, Trust |
| Dealing with tax office audits and disputes | 5% | AN | Accountability, Trust |
| Supervising and training junior staff | 5% | AN | Heart, Taste |

**Automate:** 20% (plus a good chunk of the 20% on standard returns over time)
**Assist:** 50%
**Anchor:** 30%

Moderate-to-high exposure — but with a clear path. The number-crunching shrinks. The *advising* grows. An accountant who uses AI to do their compliance work in a fraction of the time, and spends the freed-up hours being a proper advisor to small businesses — a sort of part-time finance director — becomes more valuable, not less.

The risk is for the *junior* accountant whose entire job was the data entry and standard returns. That rung of the ladder is the one wobbling.

---

### What if your score scared you?

Take a breath.

A high score doesn't mean you'll be unemployed next year. It means the *design* of your job is exposed. You aren't your job design. You're a person with experience, relationships, knowledge of your industry and the ability to learn.

Look again at your Anchor tasks. Look at your Assist tasks. That's where your future is. The goal over the next two years is to move as much of your working time as possible from the top of the list to the bottom — from Automate to Assist to Anchor.

And if your job really is almost all Automate? Then you've just found out *early*, which is the whole point of this book. You now have time to move deliberately, rather than being pushed suddenly. Chapter 11 will show you where people with your kind of experience can go.

---

### What if your score seems too low?

Be a little suspicious of yourself.

Remember the Jagged Edge and the perception trap from Chapter 3. People are generally bad at judging how much AI can do, in both directions. We tend to think *our* work is special. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it's more routine than we'd like to admit.

A good check: for every task you marked as Anchor, ask an AI assistant to try it — or to explain how it *would* do it. If it does a surprisingly good job, move that task up a category.

And one more check: think about the Doubling Clock. Your score today is a snapshot. If the length of work AI can handle keeps doubling, some Assist tasks become Automate tasks. Look at your Assist list and ask: *which of these could it do alone in three years?*

---

### The real lesson of the Task Test

Here's the thing I most want you to take from this chapter.

The people who get hurt by technological change are rarely the people who saw it coming. They're the people who assumed their whole job would either be fine or be gone, and so did nothing.

The truth is almost always in between. Some of your tasks will go. Some will get easier. Some will become more important than ever.

**The whole game is to move toward the tasks that are becoming more important — before you're forced to.**

The next five chapters go industry by industry, so you can see how this plays out in your world specifically, and where people like you can go next.

---

>

>
> **2. Write your Future Job Description.** Three or four sentences: what your job looks like when the Automate tasks are gone. (5 minutes)
>
> **3. Test one Anchor.** Pick the Anchor task you're *least* sure about and ask an AI to attempt it. Be honest about the result. (10 minutes)

# Chapter 7
## Office, Admin, Finance, Sales & Support

If you work at a desk, on a computer, processing information for most of the day, this chapter is for you. I'm going to be straight with you, because you deserve it more than you deserve comfort.

This is where AI is hitting first.

Not because office workers are less skilled. Because the work happens entirely on a screen, in words and numbers, which is exactly the material these systems are made from. The intern who's read everything is most at home in your inbox.

But "hitting first" doesn't mean "wiping out." And the story of what's happening is more interesting — and more hopeful for the right people — than most headlines suggest.

Let's start with the best real-world evidence we have.

---

### What happened when 5,000 support agents got AI

In a study later published in one of the most respected economics journals in the world, economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond followed 5,179 customer support agents at a large software company as the company rolled out an AI assistant. It suggested responses to customers in real time, and the agents could use them or not.

The results:

- On average, agents resolved about **14% more issues per hour**.
- For newer and less-skilled agents, the improvement was about **34%**.
- For the most experienced, top-performing agents, it made very little difference.
- Customers were *nicer* to agents using it. Agents were less likely to quit.

Think about what that means.

The AI had effectively learned how the best agents talked to customers — and passed those habits to everyone else. A new starter could perform like someone with months more experience.

That's wonderful news for a new starter. It's also a quiet warning. If a new person with AI can perform like an experienced one, a company may decide it doesn't need as many experienced ones. Or as many people at all.

Both of those things can be true. And that's the office work story in a nutshell.

---

### The Klarna lesson: AI-first, then human again

Klarna is a Swedish payments company — the "buy now, pay later" button you see at online checkouts.

In early 2024, Klarna announced that its AI assistant, built with OpenAI, had handled 2.3 million customer conversations in its first month — work the company said was equivalent to about **700 full-time agents**. It resolved issues in under two minutes on average, compared with eleven for humans. Klarna said it expected the assistant to add around $40 million in profit that year.

It was held up everywhere as proof that AI could replace call centres.

Then, in 2025, Klarna's CEO, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, said something unusual for a tech boss. He told Bloomberg that cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" and that the result was "lower quality." Klarna started hiring humans for customer service again.

It didn't rip out the AI. The AI still handles the bulk of simple queries. But Klarna brought people back for complex and sensitive cases, and said it was critical that customers always know they can reach a human if they want to.

This is one of the most important stories in this book, and I'll come back to it in Chapter 21. Here's what it tells office workers:

> **AI can do a huge amount of the routine work. It can't — yet — do the difficult, emotional, high-stakes or unusual work as well. And customers notice the difference.**

The jobs that remain are the ones that handle what the AI can't. So that's the work you want to be excellent at.

---

## Role-by-role breakdowns

For each role, I'll show which tasks are most exposed, which are protected, and a practical path forward. Use these alongside your own Task Test from Chapter 6.

---

### Administrative Assistants, Office Managers & Executive Assistants

**Most exposed (Automate):** Scheduling and calendar juggling. Taking and typing meeting notes. Drafting routine emails and letters. Booking travel. Filing and document formatting. Data entry.

**Changing (Assist):** Preparing reports and presentations. Researching suppliers or options. Managing projects. Preparing budgets.

**Protected (Anchor):** Being the trusted right hand who knows how the boss thinks and what matters to them. Managing relationships and office dynamics. Handling sensitive and confidential situations. Solving the physical problems of an office. Being the calm person in a crisis.

**The path forward:** The best assistants will become **operations coordinators** — people who run the AI tools that handle the scheduling and admin, and spend their time on the judgement, relationships and problem-solving. Learn to set up automations (Chapter 14). Learn basic project management. Become the person in your organisation who makes the AI tools actually work.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for routine admin roles; MODERATE for trusted EAs and office managers.**

---

### Bookkeepers, Accounts Staff & Accountants

**Most exposed:** Data entry. Bank reconciliation. Invoice processing. Accounts payable and receivable chasing. Standard tax returns. Standard financial reports. Expense checking.

**Changing:** Preparing complex returns. Financial analysis and forecasting. Audit preparation. Month-end processes.

**Protected:** Advising clients on real decisions. Complex tax planning. Handling audits and disputes with the tax office. Understanding a specific business deeply. Being the trusted advisor who can say "don't do that."

**The path forward:** Two strong directions. First, become an **advisor**: move from compliance to advice, from "here's your tax return" to "here's what I'd do if I were you." Small businesses are crying out for part-time finance directors who understand their business; AI lets you serve far more of them. Second, become an **AI finance operations specialist**: the person who sets up and manages automated finance systems for businesses. Get certified in the main accounting software platforms and their AI features.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for data entry and junior bookkeeping; MODERATE for accountants who shift toward advice.**

---

### Customer Service & Call Centre Staff

**Most exposed:** Answering common questions. Password resets, tracking orders, changing details. Routine complaints. Following scripts.

**Changing:** Complex troubleshooting (AI suggests solutions, human leads). Written responses (AI drafts, human edits).

**Protected:** Angry, upset or vulnerable customers. Complex, unusual problems. High-value customers. Situations needing discretion — breaking rules to do the right thing. Anything involving real empathy.

**The path forward:** The call centre of 2030 is likely to have fewer people, handling harder calls, for better pay — and some people who *manage* AI systems: checking what they're getting wrong, improving their responses, dealing with what they escalate. Aim to be one of those. Get good at the hardest calls. Put your hand up to help test and improve your company's AI tools. Consider moving toward **customer success** (helping business customers get value from a product), which is more relationship-based.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for tier-one support; MODERATE for complex and specialist support.**

---

### Sales

**Most exposed:** Researching prospects. Writing cold emails. Updating the customer database. Writing proposals from templates. Basic follow-ups. Scheduling meetings.

**Changing:** Preparing for meetings. Analysing which leads are worth chasing. Writing personalised proposals.

**Protected:** Building real relationships. Reading a room. Handling objections in person. Negotiating. Complex, high-value sales where trust matters. Understanding what a customer *actually* needs, not what they say they need.

**The path forward:** AI is a gift to good salespeople. It clears away the admin that eats most of a salesperson's week and lets them spend more time with people. The ones who struggle will be those whose value was mainly sending lots of emails. Lean into the human side: relationships, consultative selling, deep knowledge of your customer's industry. Use AI for every scrap of prep work.

Also watch: selling in a world of AI-generated sales emails. When every inbox is flooded with perfectly written, personalised automated emails, the salesperson who picks up the phone, turns up in person or gets a warm introduction stands out more, not less.

**Forecast: LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure for relationship sales; HIGH for high-volume transactional sales.**

---

### Human Resources

**Most exposed:** Writing job ads. Screening CVs. Scheduling interviews. Answering routine policy questions. Onboarding paperwork. Payroll admin.

**Changing:** Designing training. Analysing staff surveys and data. Writing policies.

**Protected:** Handling conflict, grievances and disciplinary matters. Supporting staff through personal crises. Making difficult calls about people. Building culture. Deciding who to hire.

**Note on hiring:** When AI helps with hiring decisions, it's one of the areas lawmakers consider "high risk." Under the EU AI Act, stricter obligations for AI used in hiring are due to apply from December 2027. Being the person who understands how to use AI in HR *fairly and legally* is valuable.

**The path forward:** Move toward **people partnering** — advising managers on handling real situations — and toward **workforce transition**, which will be a big area as companies restructure around AI. Someone has to help organisations retrain and redeploy their people well.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure for HR admin; LOW-TO-MODERATE for HR advisory roles.**

---

### Marketing Coordinators & Content Roles

(Covered in more depth in Chapter 10, but briefly:)

**Most exposed:** Writing social posts, blog posts, product descriptions and ad variations. Basic image creation. Reporting on campaign numbers.

**Protected:** Understanding customers deeply. Strategy. Brand judgement. Knowing what will actually land with *these* people. Relationships with partners and media.

**The path forward:** Move from *making* content to *directing* it. One marketer with AI can produce what a small team used to. The value shifts to deciding what's worth saying and to whom.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for content production; MODERATE for strategy and brand roles.**

---

### Insurance, Claims, Banking Back-Office & Data Processing

**Most exposed:** Processing standard claims. Checking documents. Data entry. Compliance checks against rules. Loan and application processing for straightforward cases.

**Protected:** Complex and disputed claims. Fraud investigation. Dealing with vulnerable customers — someone whose house just burned down. Decisions requiring human discretion and accountability.

**The path forward:** This is one of the most exposed areas in this book, because it's rule-based work on documents. If you're here, don't wait. Move toward complex claims, investigation, fraud (which is growing — see Chapter 16), customer advocacy or compliance oversight of AI systems. Regulators will increasingly require humans to check AI decisions about people's money. Be one of those humans.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure.**

---

### Project Managers & Team Leaders

**Most exposed:** Writing status reports. Tracking tasks. Scheduling. Taking minutes. Updating project plans.

**Protected:** Leading people. Resolving conflict. Making trade-off decisions. Managing stakeholders. Taking responsibility when things go wrong.

**The path forward:** Your job shifts toward managing a team that includes both people and AI agents. That's a new skill almost nobody has yet. Get ahead of it. (Chapter 22.)

**Forecast: LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure.**

### The three things every office worker should do this year

Whatever your specific role, three moves apply to everyone in office work:

**1. Become the AI person on your team.**
Not the tech expert. Just the person who's figured out how to use it well for your team's actual work, and can show others. In every workplace I can think of, this person becomes more valuable, more visible and harder to let go.

**2. Move toward the hard stuff.**
Volunteer for the complex cases, the difficult clients, the messy problems. The routine work is what's going. The difficult work is what's staying. Build your reputation there.

**3. Build relationships outside your company.**
If your job does shrink, the thing that gets you your next one isn't your CV. It's the people who know what you're good at. Start investing in those relationships now, while you don't need them.

# Chapter 8
## Health, Education, Law & the Licensed Professions

If you spent years training for your job — nursing school, teaching qualifications, law school, medical school, pharmacy, physio, social work — you might be reading this with a particular kind of worry.

*I gave my twenties to this. I've got the debt to prove it. Is it all about to be worth nothing?*

Here's the honest answer: probably not. But the job you trained for will change more than your training prepared you for. And the parts of it that change are, in many cases, the parts you liked least.

Let's go through it.

---

### Why licensed professions have a head start

Remember the Five Moats from Chapter 6: Hands, Heart, Trust, Taste and Accountability.

Most licensed professions have at least three.

A nurse has **Hands** (physical care), **Heart** (comfort, reassurance) and **Accountability** (a registration that can be taken away). A lawyer has **Trust**, **Taste** (judgement about strategy) and **Accountability** (professional duties to the court and client). A teacher has **Heart**, **Trust** and a room full of real children who need a real adult.

The licence itself is a moat. Society has decided that certain decisions need a named, qualified, responsible human. Changing that requires changing laws, regulations, professional standards and public trust — all of which move slowly. Very slowly.

That doesn't make you untouchable. It makes you *slower to touch*. Which means you've got time. The question is whether you use it.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — Licensed, hands-on and accountable professions see AI change the *work around the core* first — documentation, admin, research, drafting — while the core stays human through 2031.

---

## Healthcare

### What's already happening

One of the clearest early wins for AI in healthcare is the thing doctors and nurses complain about most: paperwork.

AI "scribes" listen to a consultation (with the patient's permission) and write up the notes. According to the 2026 Stanford AI Index, physicians using clinical AI tools reported spending **83% less time** writing notes.

Think about what that means. Many doctors spend a large part of their day on documentation. Giving that time back is giving time back to patients — or to doctors' own lives, which in many health systems are stretched to breaking point.

AI is also increasingly used to help read scans, flag abnormal results, check for drug interactions, summarise patient histories and triage symptoms. The research literature on AI in medicine has grown enormously.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — AI documentation tools are in real use. Diagnostic support tools are in use in some areas, with evidence still developing for many.

### Nurses

**Changing:** Documentation, handover notes, care plans, scheduling, medication checks.

**Protected:** Physical care. Assessing a patient in person. Noticing that something's "not right." Comforting frightened patients and families. Advocating for patients. Working in a team under pressure.

**Outlook:** Nursing is one of the most AI-resilient professions in this book. The physical, emotional and accountable core is enormous, and many countries face nursing shortages. AI is likely to take some paperwork off your plate. The nurses who do best will be comfortable using AI tools for documentation and monitoring, and will use the freed time well.

**Forecast: LOW exposure.**

### Doctors (GPs and specialists)

**Changing:** Note-taking. Reading test results and scans (with AI flagging). Research and keeping up to date. Writing letters and referrals. Initial triage.

**Protected:** Examining patients. Complex diagnosis across multiple conditions. Having difficult conversations. Making decisions under uncertainty. Taking responsibility. Procedures and surgery.

**Outlook:** The role shifts toward being a skilled interpreter and decision-maker who uses AI as a powerful second opinion — and toward the human conversation. Specialities focused mainly on pattern recognition in images (some parts of radiology and pathology) may see bigger shifts in how work is organised, though predictions that AI would replace radiologists have so far proven wrong. The Jagged Edge applies here powerfully: AI can be brilliant on common cases and dangerously confident on unusual ones.

**Forecast: LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure.**

### Pharmacists

**Changing:** Checking prescriptions for errors and interactions. Stock management. Routine information queries.

**Protected:** Counselling patients face-to-face. Clinical judgement on complex cases. Accountability. Vaccinations and in-person services.

**Outlook:** The dispensing and checking side is increasingly automated. The clinical and advisory side grows. Pharmacists who lean into consultation and clinical services do well.

**Forecast: MODERATE exposure.**

### Medical admin, billing and coding

**Outlook:** This is where healthcare's highest exposure sits. Medical coding, billing, prior authorisations, appointment booking and records management are rule-based information tasks. If you work here, treat Chapter 7's advice on admin and claims as yours too, and look at moving toward patient-facing coordination roles or clinical support training.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure.**

### Allied health (physio, OT, speech therapy, psychology, dental)

**Outlook:** Largely physical and relational. AI helps with notes, exercise programs, assessments and admin. Talk-based therapies face a particular question — AI chatbots offering mental health support — which I cover in Chapter 17. My view: AI tools may widen access to basic support, but the trusted human relationship at the heart of therapy remains hard to replicate, and the risks of getting it wrong are serious.

**Forecast: LOW exposure (physical); LOW-TO-MODERATE (talk-based).**

---

## Education

### What's happening with students

This is where AI has moved fastest in everyday life. According to the Stanford AI Index 2026, about **80% of American high school and college students** use AI for school. Only about **6% of teachers** said they had clear policies on it.

Read those two numbers again. That's an entire generation working out how to use a powerful tool largely on their own, while the adults responsible for their education try to catch up.

### What AI can do for learning

It can be extraordinary. Two careful studies, published in 2025:

**Nigeria:** A World Bank randomised trial gave secondary students six weeks of after-school English tutoring using an AI assistant (Microsoft Copilot, built on GPT-4), guided by teachers. The learning gains were equivalent to roughly **one and a half to two years** of normal schooling. The gains showed up in end-of-year exams too.

**Harvard:** Physics students using a carefully designed AI tutor learned **more than twice as much** as students in an active-learning class run by experienced instructors — and in less time.

The critical detail in both: the AI was *designed and supervised to make students think*, not to give them answers. In the Harvard case, the tutor was built on proven teaching principles and specifically set up not to just hand over solutions.

Without that design, the same tools can let students skip the thinking entirely. I call that the **Lazy/Smart Fork**, and it's the centre of Chapter 19.

### Teachers

**Changing:** Lesson planning. Creating worksheets and resources. Marking routine work. Writing reports. Differentiating material for different levels. Admin.

**Protected:** Managing a classroom. Building relationships with kids. Noticing the child who's struggling at home. Motivating the unmotivated. Being a role model. Pastoral care. Teaching kids *how* to think, and how to use AI wisely.

**Outlook:** I'm optimistic here. Teachers are drowning in admin, and AI can take a lot of it. The job likely shifts toward being a coach, mentor, guide and designer of learning — with AI tutors helping individual students practise between lessons. The teachers who struggle will be those who try to ignore AI or ban it outright; students will use it anyway.

A teacher who becomes excellent at designing AI-supported learning — who knows how to use AI to make students think harder rather than less — is going to be in demand.

**Forecast: LOW exposure for classroom teachers; MODERATE for tutoring, online course creation and some lecturing roles.**

### Tutors

A special mention, because this is a genuinely exposed area. Basic homework help and exam-prep tutoring is something AI does cheaply and at any hour. Tutors who survive will offer what AI can't: accountability, motivation, a relationship, working with kids who have particular needs, and teaching families how to use AI well.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure.**

---

## Law

### What's happening

Law is made of documents, and AI is very good with documents. Legal research, contract review, summarising cases, drafting standard letters and agreements — AI tools are already widely used in larger firms.

But law has also produced some of the most famous AI failures. Lawyers in several countries have been caught submitting court documents that cited cases the AI made up. Judges have imposed penalties. It's a perfect illustration of the Jagged Edge: AI writes a convincing legal argument, and some of it is fiction.

### Lawyers

**Changing:** Legal research. Document review. Drafting standard contracts and letters. Due diligence. Summarising.

**Protected:** Advising clients on strategy. Negotiation. Advocacy in court. Judgement on novel situations. Client relationships. Professional accountability.

**Outlook:** The traditional law firm model — lots of junior lawyers billing hours for research and document review, a few senior partners doing strategy and client work — is under real pressure. The bottom of that pyramid is exactly the work AI does well. Expect fewer junior roles, more pressure on hourly billing, and more value in judgement and relationships.

There's a new-demand story here too. Legal help is too expensive for most ordinary people. If AI makes it much cheaper, far more people might get legal help who currently go without. That could create a lot of work for lawyers willing to serve everyday clients efficiently.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for junior/research-heavy roles; MODERATE for experienced advisory and court work.**

### Paralegals and legal secretaries

**Outlook:** Among the most exposed roles in law. Move toward client-facing coordination, managing AI legal tools, specialist areas like conveyancing oversight or compliance, or retraining toward areas with more human contact.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure.**

## Other licensed and professional roles

### Engineers (civil, structural, mechanical, electrical)

AI helps with calculations, drafting, design options and documentation. The engineer who signs off that a design is safe — and who understands the physical world, the site, the regulations and the risks — remains central. **LOW-TO-MODERATE exposure.**

### Architects

AI can generate designs and visualisations quickly. The architect's value moves toward understanding clients, sites, regulations, buildability and taste. **MODERATE exposure.**

### Financial advisors

AI can build portfolios and answer questions. Trust, behavioural coaching ("don't sell everything in a panic") and life planning remain human. **MODERATE exposure.**

### Social workers, counsellors, clergy, community workers

Deeply human work built on Heart and Trust. AI helps with admin and case notes. **LOW exposure.**

### Police, emergency services, defence

Physical, accountable, high-stakes and human. AI will be used heavily for analysis and surveillance — which raises its own serious questions (Chapter 20). **LOW exposure** for frontline roles.

### The trap for professionals

Here's the risk I most want professionals to hear.

It's not that AI replaces you. It's that **a professional who uses AI well replaces a professional who doesn't.**

A GP who uses an AI scribe sees more patients, with better notes, and goes home on time. A lawyer who uses AI research tools produces better advice faster and charges less. A teacher who uses AI to personalise learning gets better results.

If you're a professional and you're waiting for your professional body to tell you how to use AI, you'll be waiting a while, and colleagues who didn't wait will pull ahead.

---

### The path for professionals

**1. Use AI on your admin now.** Documentation, letters, research, summaries. Get your time back. Within professional and privacy rules — check your organisation's policies, and never put identifiable patient or client information into tools that aren't approved for it.

**2. Learn where the Jagged Edge is in your field.** Your professional knowledge is what lets you catch AI's mistakes. That's increasingly what you're paid for.

**3. Lean into the human core.** The conversation, the judgement, the physical skill, the accountability. That's your moat. Get even better at it.

**4. Become the bridge.** Every profession needs people who understand both the professional work and the AI tools. Be one of them. Help write your organisation's AI policy. Lead the trial.

>
> **2. Use an approved AI tool on one piece of admin** this week — a letter, a lesson plan, a summary — without including any private personal information. (15 minutes)
>
> **3. Write down three mistakes AI would likely make in your field** that only a trained professional would catch. That's your value, on paper. (10 minutes)

# Chapter 9
## Trades, Transport, Factories & Warehouses

If you work with your hands, you've probably spent the last few years hearing about AI and thinking: *good luck getting a chatbot to fix a burst pipe at 2am.*

You're right.

And for most of this book's five-year window, you're going to stay right. But there's more to this chapter than "you're safe." There are three stories here — one about robots, one about a boom that's happening *because* of AI, and one about the biggest opportunity sitting right in front of tradespeople that most of them are ignoring.

---

### Story one: the robots are real, and they're slower than the videos

You've seen the videos. Humanoid robots doing backflips. Robot hands folding laundry. Robot dogs climbing stairs. Sleek machines walking through factories.

Here's what's actually going on, as of 2026.

**Humanoid robots** — the ones shaped like people — are in real trials in factories and warehouses. Companies like Figure and Agility Robotics have robots doing paid work for customers: Figure's robots have been loading sheet-metal parts at a BMW plant in South Carolina, and Agility's Digit robots have been moving boxes and totes in warehouses. But as of mid-2026, independent trackers of the industry reported that no humanoid robot maker had sustained commercial deployments beyond the low hundreds of units. Tesla has built many of its Optimus robots, but Elon Musk himself described them in early 2026 as being mainly for learning and data collection rather than productive work.

**In homes,** robots are far behind. The Stanford AI Index 2026 found robots succeeded at only around **12% of household tasks** they were tested on.

Why is the physical world so hard? Because it's messy. A factory can be designed around a robot: same parts, same place, same lighting, every time. A house built in 1962 with a crawl space full of spider webs, corroded fittings, a wall that isn't quite straight and a dog barking at you is a completely different problem. Human hands, eyes and judgement handle that kind of chaos without thinking. Machines, so far, can't.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Humanoid robots are in early commercial trials; deployments are small.
> **POSSIBLE** — Robots doing meaningful amounts of paid work in structured environments (warehouses, factories) at real scale by 2029–2031.
> **WILD CARD** — General-purpose robots doing skilled trades work in homes and on building sites within this five-year window.

---

### Driving is different — it's already happening

One area where machines really *are* doing the physical job: driving.

Waymo, the self-driving company owned by Google's parent Alphabet, was providing around **500,000 paid rides per week** across American cities by early-to-mid 2026 — ten times what it was doing two years earlier — with no one in the driver's seat. It was expanding city by city through 2026 and aiming for a million rides a week by the end of the year.

In trucking, Aurora Innovation began running driverless heavy trucks commercially on Texas highways in 2025, first between Dallas and Houston, then on longer routes, and was aiming for hundreds of driverless trucks on the road by the end of 2026.

This is the clearest example of AI taking over a physical job. And it's worth understanding *how* it's happening: **city by city, route by route, in good weather first, on well-mapped roads first.** Not everywhere at once.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Driverless taxis and some driverless trucking are operating commercially in parts of the US.
> **LIKELY** — Continued expansion to more cities and highway corridors through 2031.
> **POSSIBLE** — Significant reduction in demand for taxi, rideshare and long-haul drivers in certain regions by 2031.

---

## Role-by-role breakdowns

### Electricians

**AI touches:** Quoting, invoicing, scheduling, reading plans, fault-finding help (describe a problem, get likely causes), compliance paperwork, marketing.

**Protected:** Basically everything that involves being on site. Wiring, testing, diagnosing real faults in real buildings, safety sign-off.

**The bonus:** Electricians are one of the biggest *winners* of the AI boom. More on that below.

**Forecast: VERY LOW exposure. HIGH opportunity.**

### Plumbers, gasfitters, HVAC technicians

**AI touches:** Quoting, admin, customer communication, diagnosing from photos and descriptions, marketing.

**Protected:** The work itself. Every house is different. Every job is a puzzle solved with hands.

**The bonus:** HVAC and cooling are in massive demand from data centres.

**Forecast: VERY LOW exposure. HIGH opportunity for HVAC.**

### Builders, carpenters, roofers, bricklayers, tilers, painters

**AI touches:** Estimating, project planning, ordering materials, design visualisation for clients, admin, marketing.

**Protected:** The physical build.

**Watch:** Prefabrication and factory-built modular housing could grow, which moves some construction work into factories where automation is easier. That's a slow shift, not a sudden one.

**Forecast: LOW exposure.**

### Mechanics and automotive technicians

**AI touches:** Diagnostics (increasingly AI-assisted), parts ordering, customer communication, service records.

**Protected:** Physical repair.

**Watch:** Electric vehicles have fewer moving parts and different skills. Mechanics who get qualified on EVs and high-voltage systems are positioning themselves well.

**Forecast: LOW exposure. MODERATE change (EV transition).**

### Cleaners

**AI touches:** Scheduling, booking, quoting, marketing.

**Protected:** Most cleaning in homes and varied commercial spaces. Robot vacuums exist and are getting better, but the full range of cleaning a person does — bathrooms, kitchens, clutter, judgement about what matters to the client — remains very hard for robots.

**Watch:** Large, open, simple commercial spaces (warehouses, airports, shopping centres) are where robotic floor cleaning is growing.

**Forecast: LOW exposure (homes); MODERATE (large commercial floor areas).**

### Truck drivers

**What's happening:** Driverless trucks are operating on some US highway routes. This is real.

**Likely pattern:** Long, straight highway runs between big depots are automated first. Local delivery, city driving, complicated loading, bad weather, rural roads and specialist freight (dangerous goods, oversize loads, livestock) stay human much longer.

**Honest outlook:** If you're a long-haul highway driver, especially in the US Sun Belt, this is the most directly exposed trades-and-transport role in this chapter. It won't happen everywhere overnight — it'll spread route by route — but it's worth planning now.

**Transitions:** Local and specialist driving. Fleet coordination and logistics. Supervising autonomous trucks from terminals. Diesel and EV mechanics. Dispatching. Heavy equipment operation. Your knowledge of roads, loads and logistics is valuable — it needs a new home before it needs one urgently.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure (long-haul highway); LOW-TO-MODERATE (local, specialist).**

### Taxi and rideshare drivers

**What's happening:** Robotaxis are operating commercially in a growing number of American cities.

**Honest outlook:** If you drive in a city where Waymo or a competitor is operating, watch closely. Elsewhere, you likely have more time — but the direction is clear.

**Transitions:** Specialist transport (patients, elderly, disability transport where care and assistance matter), delivery that involves more than dropping a package, logistics roles, or retraining into trades.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure in cities with robotaxis; MODERATE elsewhere by 2031.**

### Warehouse workers

**What's happening:** Warehouses are among the most automated workplaces already — conveyor systems, sorting machines, robots that carry shelves to workers. Humanoid and mobile robots are being trialled for picking and moving.

**Protected (for now):** Picking unusual items, handling damaged goods, problem-solving, maintenance, anything requiring flexibility.

**Outlook:** Continued gradual automation. The growth jobs are in **maintaining, fixing and supervising** the machines. Demand for robotics technicians has been rising sharply.

**Transitions:** Robotics and automation technician (often via a short course or apprenticeship). Maintenance. Logistics coordination.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure.**

### Factory and manufacturing workers

**Outlook:** Manufacturing has been automating for decades; AI and robotics continue that. Repetitive, predictable work in controlled environments is most exposed. Skilled trades within manufacturing — maintenance fitters, electricians, toolmakers, technicians — remain in demand and often short-staffed.

**Forecast: MODERATE exposure (repetitive roles); LOW (skilled maintenance and technical roles).**

---

### Story two: the boom nobody expected

Here's the plot twist in the AI story that almost nobody saw coming.

Artificial intelligence runs on enormous buildings full of computers called **data centres**. Those buildings need electricity — a staggering amount of it. The International Energy Agency expects global data centre electricity use to roughly double by 2030, to about the amount Japan uses today.

Someone has to build those buildings. Wire them. Cool them. Build the power lines to them. Maintain the generators. Build the new power stations and grid connections.

That someone isn't a chatbot.

In a 2026 survey by the Associated General Contractors of America, **58% of construction firms** said data centre projects were increasing competition for workers, and nearly half said they felt pressure to raise wages. Reports through 2026 described data centre electricians in high-demand areas earning very high hourly rates, with overtime on top.

Put simply: AI is creating a gold rush, and the people selling the shovels are electricians, HVAC technicians, linesmen, construction workers, welders and engineers.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — AI infrastructure is driving strong demand for skilled trades in construction, electrical and cooling work.
> **LIKELY** — This continues through at least 2030, based on announced investment and energy projections.

For young people choosing a path, and for anyone thinking about retraining, this deserves serious attention. An electrical apprenticeship might be one of the most AI-proof, high-demand career choices available right now. I don't say that lightly.

---

### Story three: the opportunity tradies are ignoring

Here's the thing I most want tradespeople to hear.

AI is not coming for your *work*. But it's the best thing that's ever happened to your *business*.

Ask any self-employed tradie what they hate about their job, and I'd bet it's not the tools. It's:

- Writing quotes at 9pm.
- Chasing invoices.
- Answering the same phone questions twenty times a week.
- Trying to keep up with social media.
- Writing up job notes and compliance paperwork.
- Scheduling around cancellations.
- Doing the books.

AI can help with nearly every one of those, today.

- Talk a quote into your phone on the drive home; have AI turn it into a professional written quote.
- Take photos of a finished job; have AI write the Facebook post and the Google Business update.
- Set up an AI assistant to answer common customer questions and book jobs.
- Use AI to write up compliance notes from a voice memo.
- Use accounting software with AI features to match receipts and chase invoices.

Think about what that does. The tradie who gets their admin down from ten hours a week to three isn't just less stressed. They can take more jobs, respond faster, look more professional and win more work than the one down the road still writing quotes on the back of an envelope.

This is the lowest-hanging fruit in the entire book. If you're a tradesperson, Chapter 14 and the 30-day challenge in Chapter 15 will give you the practical how-to.

### The honest risk for trades

I don't want to give you a false sense of security. So here's the realistic risk for trades over this five-year window.

**It's not robots. It's other people.**

If AI disrupts a lot of office jobs, some of those workers will retrain into trades. That's more competition for apprenticeships and, eventually, for work. Meanwhile, if white-collar incomes are squeezed in a downturn, people may put off renovations and non-urgent jobs.

So the smart move for trades isn't to relax. It's to:

1. **Get the highest qualification you can** in your trade — licences and specialisations are moats.
2. **Specialise** in areas with growing demand: electrical, solar and batteries, EV charging, HVAC and cooling, data centre work, smart home systems.
3. **Use AI to run a tighter business** than your competitors.
4. **Build your reputation and reviews** — trust is a moat, and online reviews are how trust scales.
5. **Consider training others.** If people move into trades, someone has to train them.

# Chapter 10
## Creative & Tech Work

For most of history, the jobs people assumed were safest from machines were the creative ones and the clever ones. Machines would take the boring, repetitive work, and leave the art, the writing, the design, the coding and the invention to us.

It turned out almost exactly the other way round.

The first jobs to feel real pressure from modern AI weren't factory jobs. They were copywriters, illustrators, translators, junior coders and stock photographers. The robots came for the poets before the plumbers.

If you do creative or technical work, that's been a strange and sometimes painful thing to watch. So let's talk about it honestly — and then talk about why, for the right people, this might also be the best time in history to be a creator or a builder.

---

### Why creative and tech work got hit first

Go back to the intern who's read everything. What has that intern read? Billions of words of writing. Millions of images and their descriptions. Enormous amounts of computer code.

So it's no surprise that producing *more* writing, images and code is where it's strongest. It's working with exactly the materials it learned from.

In software, the change has been dramatic. On one widely used test — which checks whether AI can fix real bugs taken from real software projects — the best systems went from solving only a small fraction of problems in 2023 to solving the large majority by 2025–2026. The Stanford AI Index 2026 reported that employment of software developers aged 22 to 25 had fallen by nearly 20% since 2024.

In writing and design, the change has been messier to measure but just as real for many freelancers. Rates for routine content writing, basic graphic design, stock imagery and simple translation have come under heavy pressure, because clients can now get "good enough" for almost nothing.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Routine creative and coding work is already significantly affected, especially for juniors and freelancers doing volume work.

---

### The line that matters: making things vs. making things matter

Here's the idea I want creators to hold onto:

> **AI has made it nearly free to make things. It hasn't made it any easier to make things *matter*.**

Anyone can now produce a blog post, a logo, a jingle, a video, an app. That means there's an ocean of *stuff*. And when there's an ocean of stuff, three things become much more valuable:

**1. Taste** — knowing what's good, what's right for *this* audience, what to cut, what's worth making at all.

**2. Voice** — a recognisable human perspective that people come back to because it's *you*.

**3. Trust and connection** — an audience that knows you, a client who relies on you, a reputation that means something.

A machine can generate a thousand logos. A great designer knows which one — and more importantly, can sit with a client, understand what their business actually stands for, and make them *feel* understood. That's the job now.

---

## Role-by-role breakdowns

### Software developers

**Most exposed:** Writing routine code. Boilerplate. Simple bug fixes. Writing tests. Converting code between languages. Basic web and app building. Documentation.

**Changing:** Building features (AI writes much of the code, developer directs, reviews and fixes). Debugging complex problems.

**Protected:** Understanding what the business actually needs. System design and architecture. Security. Reviewing AI-written code for subtle problems (the Jagged Edge again — AI-written code can look right and hide bugs). Working with people to figure out what to build. Taking responsibility for systems that must not fail.

**What's happening to careers:** The squeeze is at the bottom. Juniors are finding it hard to get hired because much of what juniors used to do, AI now does. Experienced developers who can design systems and direct AI are, so far, doing well.

**The path forward:**
- If you're junior: build real things and show them. A portfolio of working projects beats a degree certificate. Learn to use AI coding tools brilliantly — you're competing against people who do. And get deep in one domain (health, finance, logistics, whatever), because *understanding a business problem* is what makes you more than a code-writer.
- If you're experienced: get fluent with AI agents that code. Your job becomes reviewing, directing and designing. You might do the output of a whole team.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for junior/routine development; MODERATE for senior engineers who direct AI.**

---

### Writers, copywriters & content creators

**Most exposed:** SEO blog posts. Product descriptions. Generic marketing copy. Social media filler. Basic press releases. Summaries.

**Protected:** Original reporting and research. A distinctive voice. Deep subject knowledge. Storytelling from real experience. Strategy — knowing what to say and why. Relationships with editors, clients and readers.

**Honest outlook:** The market for "words by the kilo" has largely collapsed. The market for genuinely good, original, trusted writing may actually be stronger, because readers are drowning in bland AI text and crave something real.

**The path forward:** Move up the value chain. From writer to **editor and strategist**. From generic to **specialist** (one field you know deeply). From anonymous to **known** (build a name and an audience). Use AI for research, first drafts and grunt work; bring the voice, the judgement and the truth yourself.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for volume content; LOW-TO-MODERATE for distinctive, expert or original work.**

---

### Graphic designers & illustrators

**Most exposed:** Stock-style images. Simple social graphics. Basic logos. Resizing and variations. Mock-ups.

**Protected:** Brand strategy and identity work. Understanding clients. Art direction. Distinctive personal styles people seek out. Anything where the human artist *is* the value.

**Note:** There are serious and unresolved legal and ethical questions about AI image tools trained on artists' work without permission. Courts and legislators in several countries are working through them. How they're resolved could significantly change this market.

**The path forward:** Become an **art director** — the person who guides AI tools and human work toward a coherent vision. Build a distinctive style. Get closer to clients and business strategy. Offer "human-made" as a feature where clients value it (Chapter 21).

**Forecast: HIGH exposure for routine design; MODERATE for brand and art direction.**

---

### Video editors, photographers & musicians

**Video:** AI tools increasingly handle cutting, captioning, colour and even generating footage. Human value: storytelling, pacing, emotional judgement, live shoots, working with real people. **MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure** for routine editing.

**Photography:** Stock photography is heavily exposed. Weddings, events, portraits, journalism — anything where a real moment with real people is captured — much less so. People want real photos of their real wedding. **HIGH (stock) / LOW (events, portraits).**

**Musicians:** AI can generate background music, jingles and generic tracks. Live performance, a real artist people connect with, and original artistic vision remain human. **HIGH (library/background music) / LOW (live, artist-led).**

---

### Marketers

**Most exposed:** Producing campaign content. Ad variations. Reporting. Basic analysis. Email sequences.

**Protected:** Understanding customers. Strategy. Brand. Creative direction. Knowing what will actually move people. Building partnerships.

**Outlook:** The Stanford AI Index highlighted large productivity gains in marketing output among organisations that have built AI into their workflows. One strong marketer with AI can now do what a small team did. That means fewer marketing jobs doing production — and more value in people who understand customers deeply and can direct the machine.

**Forecast: HIGH exposure (production); MODERATE (strategy).**

---

### Data analysts

**Most exposed:** Writing queries. Building standard dashboards. Cleaning data. Producing routine reports.

**Protected:** Asking the right questions. Understanding the business context. Telling a clear story from the numbers. Knowing when data is misleading. Convincing people to act.

**Outlook:** AI now lets managers ask questions of their data in plain English. The analyst who only produced reports is exposed. The analyst who helps the business make better decisions is more valuable than ever.

**Forecast: MODERATE-TO-HIGH exposure.**

---

### The creator's opportunity

Now the good news. And it's genuinely good.

For all of history, making things at scale needed money, equipment and teams. A film needed a crew. An app needed developers. A magazine needed a printing press. A business needed staff.

AI is collapsing those costs.

A single person with good ideas, taste and AI tools can now:

- write, design and publish a book;
- build a working app or website;
- produce a professional-looking video series;
- run a small media business;
- create and sell digital products;
- design a brand from scratch.

The barrier is no longer "can I afford a team?" It's "do I have something worth saying, and can I get people to care?"

That's a very different barrier. It rewards originality, persistence, real expertise and genuine connection with an audience. It rewards people who've lived something and can speak to others who are living it.

> **The future doesn't belong to people who can make things. Machines can make things. It belongs to people who know what's worth making, and who other people trust to make it.**

---

> ### DO THIS NOW
>
> **1. Name your taste.** Write down three things in your field that you think are *genuinely good* and three that are rubbish — and why. That "why" is the thing AI can't give you. Get better at articulating it. (10 minutes)
>
> **2. Build one thing with AI this week** that you couldn't have built alone: a simple web page, a short video, a small tool, a designed PDF. (1–2 hours)
>
> **3. Start showing your work publicly.** One post, one portfolio piece, one project. Visibility is a moat. (30 minutes)

# Chapter 11
## If You Do X, Start Learning Y

This is the chapter I'd want to hand to someone who's just done the Task Test, got a high score, and is sitting at the kitchen table thinking: *okay, so now what?*

Here's the most important thing I can tell you about changing direction:

> **You are not starting from zero. You never are.**

Every job you've done has given you things that transfer. Knowledge of an industry. Understanding of customers. Skills with people. Problem-solving habits. Reliability. Things you know that you don't even realise you know, because you've known them so long.

The trick of a good career change isn't to throw all that away and start again. It's to find the *next door* — a role where your existing experience is an advantage, and you only need to add a few new skills to walk through it.

This chapter is a map of those doors.

---

### How to read the Reskill Map

For each current role, I'll give you:

- **Your transferable strengths** — what you already have.
- **Next doors** — roles where those strengths give you a head start.
- **Learn** — the specific skills to add.
- **First step** — something you could start this month.

These aren't the only options. They're starting points to get you thinking. And a few honest cautions:

- Some of these new roles are still emerging and job titles vary a lot between companies and countries.
- Pay and demand vary by where you live. Check job listings in your area before committing to anything expensive.
- **Be very careful with expensive courses.** There's a gold rush of "AI certifications" of wildly varying quality. Many of the best learning resources are free or cheap. (See the Resources section at the end of the book.)

### Proof Over Paper

There's one principle that applies to every door on this map.

In a world where AI can write a flawless CV, a perfect cover letter and a professional-sounding LinkedIn profile for anyone in thirty seconds, **paper claims are losing value.**

What's gaining value is **proof**.

- A portfolio of real work.
- A project people can see.
- A result you can point to: "I set up this system and it saved the team six hours a week."
- A reference from someone the employer trusts.
- A small business with real customers.
- A published article, a working app, a before-and-after.

Certificates still matter for some things, particularly licensed professions and trades. But for most career changes, **one real project is worth three online certificates.**

So when you're choosing your next door, don't only ask "what course should I do?" Ask: **"What can I build, fix, help with or produce in the next three months that proves I can do this?"**

---

### The three-question test before you spend money on training

Before you pay for any course, ask:

1. **Do people in the job I want actually list this qualification in job ads?** (Check ten real job listings.)
2. **Can I learn the first 20% for free** to test whether I even like it? (Usually yes.)
3. **What will I be able to *show* at the end?** (If the answer is "a certificate" and nothing else, be cautious.)

# Chapter 12
## The Income Tripod

Let's talk about the thing that actually keeps people awake.

Not "will AI become conscious?" Not "will robots take over?"

*Can I pay the rent if something happens to my job?*

That's the real fear under most of the AI anxiety out there. It's not abstract. It's the mortgage, the kids' school shoes, the car loan, the groceries that cost more every month. It's the knot in your stomach when you look at your bank balance two days before payday.

So this chapter isn't about AI at all, really. It's about making your household strong enough that whatever AI does — slow, steady or fast — you have *time* and *choices*.

Because that's what financial resilience really is. Not being rich. Having time and choices when things change.

*(A quick, necessary note: I'm not a financial advisor, and this chapter is general education, not personal advice. Your situation is yours. For big decisions — especially about debt, investing, tax or retirement — talk to a qualified, independent professional.)*

---

### Why a table needs three legs

Picture a stool with one leg. You can balance on it, if you're careful and nothing moves. Most households live like this: one main income (or two incomes from the same kind of work), and nothing much underneath.

Knock that one leg, and you're on the floor.

Now picture a stool with three legs. Knock one, and it wobbles — but it stands. You've got time to fix the broken leg.

**The Income Tripod** is the idea that every household, over the next five years, should be working toward three legs:

**Leg 1: Your main income** — your job or main business. Made as secure as possible (Parts III and V of this book).

**Leg 2: A skill-based second income** — something that earns money from a *different* source than your main job, even if it's small. (Chapter 13.)

**Leg 3: A cash cushion** — money set aside that buys you time if Leg 1 or Leg 2 breaks.

You don't need to build all three this month. Most people won't have all three solid for a year or two. But knowing that this is the shape you're building toward changes every financial decision you make.

Let's start with the leg that matters most, and that most people skip.

---

## Leg 3: The Cash Cushion

### Why it matters more now

When a job disappears, the damage isn't just the lost income. It's the *speed*. If you have no savings, you have to take the first thing that comes along — even if it's worse paid, a worse fit, or a job that's also about to be automated. You can't afford to retrain. You can't afford to wait for the right thing. Panic makes decisions for you.

A cushion changes that. Three months of expenses saved means that if your job goes, you can breathe, look properly, maybe take a short course, and make a *good* decision rather than a desperate one.

In every one of the three futures from Chapter 5, a cushion helps. In the Fast Break future, it could be the most important thing you did.

### How big?

The standard advice from most financial educators is to aim for **three to six months of essential expenses**. Not income — *essential expenses*: rent or mortgage, food, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, transport.

If that number looks impossible, I understand. For a lot of families it is, right now. So here's a ladder:

1. **$500–$1,000** — enough to stop a car repair or a dental bill from becoming debt.
2. **One month of essential expenses.**
3. **Three months.**
4. **Six months** — especially if you're in a high-exposure job, self-employed, or the only earner.

Each rung makes you meaningfully safer than the one below it. Don't be discouraged by the top rung. Climb the next one.

### How to actually build it

- **Automate it.** Set up an automatic transfer to a separate savings account on payday, even a small amount. What you don't see, you don't spend.
- **Keep it separate and boring.** A high-interest savings account at a different bank from your everyday account works well for many people — easy enough to get to in an emergency, awkward enough that you won't dip into it for takeaway.
- **Feed it windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses, birthday money, side income — a chunk goes straight to the cushion until it's full.
- **Don't invest your emergency cushion in things that can crash.** The point is that it's *there* when you need it. Shares and crypto can fall exactly when the economy — and your job — is under pressure.

## The Vulnerability Audit

Before you can strengthen your household, you need to know where it's weak. Answer these honestly. Tick each one that applies.

**Income vulnerability**
☐ More than 80% of our household income comes from one job.
☐ Two incomes, but both in high-exposure jobs (Task Test score over 40% Automate).
☐ My main income is in an industry already shrinking.
☐ I have no income source outside my main job.

**Cost vulnerability**
☐ Our fixed costs (rent/mortgage, loans, subscriptions) are more than 60% of take-home pay.
☐ We have high-interest debt (credit cards, payday loans, buy-now-pay-later).
☐ We'd have to sell something important if we lost one income for three months.

**Cushion vulnerability**
☐ We have less than one month of expenses saved.
☐ Our savings are all in investments that could fall in value.

**Protection vulnerability**
☐ We don't have income protection or life insurance, and people depend on us.
☐ We haven't checked what unemployment support we'd be entitled to.

**Skill vulnerability**
☐ I haven't learned a significant new skill in two years.
☐ I don't use AI in my work at all.

**Score:** Count your ticks.
- **0–3:** Solid foundation. Focus on building Leg 2.
- **4–7:** Some real weak spots. Pick the top two and start there.
- **8+:** You're exposed. Not doomed — exposed. Start with the cushion and high-interest debt, and work through this chapter step by step.

---

### Fix the leaks: high-interest debt

If you have credit card debt, payday loans or buy-now-pay-later balances charging high interest, that debt is quietly working against you every month.

In a period of uncertainty, high-interest debt is dangerous for a simple reason: if your income drops, the debt doesn't. It keeps growing.

A common approach financial educators recommend:
1. Build a small starter cushion first ($500–$1,000), so emergencies don't create *new* debt.
2. Then throw everything extra at the highest-interest debt while paying minimums on the rest.
3. Once that's gone, roll the payment onto the next.

If debt is overwhelming, speak to a free, non-profit financial counselling service. Most countries have them. They're genuinely helpful and they won't judge you.

---

### Lower your fixed costs (the underrated move)

People focus on earning more. But in uncertain times, **lower fixed costs are a form of freedom.**

Every dollar of fixed monthly costs you remove is a dollar you don't need to earn, every month, forever. It lowers the income you need to survive, which means more jobs become viable, more career changes become possible, and a smaller cushion covers more months.

Go through your bank statement line by line. Ask of every regular payment: *would I sign up for this again today?*

(AI can help. Export your last three months of transactions — with account numbers removed — and ask an AI to categorise them and find recurring charges. Many people find subscriptions they'd forgotten about.)

## If unemployment rises: the household downturn plan

This is the plan you write *now*, while things are calm, so you're not writing it in a panic.

Sit down with your partner, or on your own, and write answers to these:

**1. Early warning signs for our household.** What would tell us our income is at risk? (Hiring freeze at work. Restructure announced. Team not being replaced. Industry news. Your Task Test score.)

**2. What we'd cut first.** List the costs you'd cut immediately if income dropped. Decide now, so it's not an argument later.

**3. What we'd protect.** Housing, food, kids' essentials, insurance. In that order.

**4. Who we'd call.** People who might know of work. Recruiters. Former colleagues. Your network (Chapter 7 told you to start building it).

**5. What we're entitled to.** Look up now what unemployment benefits, redundancy entitlements and hardship assistance exist where you live. Your employment contract. Any income protection insurance you have.

**6. What we'd do in the first week.** Update your CV and portfolio. Apply for benefits immediately. Contact your lender early if you might struggle with repayments (many have hardship arrangements, and talking early helps). Start the 30-day challenge (Chapter 15) if you haven't.

Write it on one page. Put it somewhere you'll find it. Hope you never need it.

## What about Universal Basic Income?

You'll hear a lot about **Universal Basic Income** — the idea that governments could give everyone a regular payment, no strings attached, especially if AI takes a lot of jobs.

It's worth understanding what the evidence says, so you can think clearly about it.

The largest recent study in the US was run by a non-profit called OpenResearch, backed by Sam Altman, the head of OpenAI. For three years, 1,000 lower-income people received **$1,000 a month**, no strings attached. A comparison group of 2,000 received $50 a month.

What happened?

- Recipients worked **slightly less** on average — but the researchers found evidence this was about having more choice (for example, being pickier about jobs, or spending time on family), not about people not wanting to work.
- They were **more likely to report business ideas** and plans to start a business.
- Stress and food insecurity improved noticeably — but mainly **in the first year**, and those improvements faded.
- There were **no significant effects on physical health**.
- Parents spent more on their children.

So: not a miracle, not a disaster. A modest, meaningful help for people on low incomes.

Would governments introduce something like this if AI caused widespread job losses? Possibly. Serious people from across the political spectrum have argued for versions of it. Others argue strongly against it on cost and other grounds. Other ideas — stronger unemployment insurance, retraining accounts, shorter working weeks, taxes on automation — are also being debated.

> **FORECAST LABEL: POSSIBLE** — New forms of income support are introduced in some countries in response to AI disruption by 2031.

My honest advice: **don't plan your family's future around it.** If support arrives, great. It'll be a floor, not a lifestyle, and it'll likely arrive after the pain rather than before. Build your own tripod first.

## Earning With AI: From $0, $100 and $1,000

Let me start by making you a promise about this chapter. It's the chapter most likely to contain rubbish in other AI books, so I want to be clear.

**There is no secret AI money machine.**

There's no prompt that prints cash. There's no "passive income system" that makes $10,000 a month while you sleep, set up in a weekend. If someone is selling you one, they're making their money from *you*, not from the system.

What there *is*, right now, is a real and unusual opportunity. And it's much more ordinary, and much more achievable, than the hype.

Here it is:

> **Most businesses and most people have no idea how to use AI well. You can learn it faster than they can. And people will pay you to help them.**

That's it. That's the opportunity. It's not glamorous. It works.

Remember the MIT study from Chapter 1 — the one suggesting that the vast majority of corporate AI pilots weren't producing measurable results? That's not just a story about hype. It's a story about a *gap*. Businesses are spending money on AI and not getting value, because they don't know how to fit it into how they actually work.

Every gap is a job.

### The three kinds of AI income

Almost every realistic way to earn with AI falls into one of three buckets.

**1. AI-Boosted Services** — You do a service people already pay for, but AI lets you do it faster, better or cheaper. You keep more of the money or serve more people.
*Examples: writing, bookkeeping, marketing, design, virtual assistance, tutoring, research, translation-with-review, cleaning business run more efficiently.*

**2. AI Setup & Help** — You help businesses or people use AI themselves. You're the bridge.
*Examples: setting up AI tools for a local business; training staff; building simple automations; teaching older people to use AI safely; helping families with AI safety (Chapter 18).*

**3. Human-Premium Services** — Things people will pay *more* for because a real human does them. As AI makes everything else cheap, these become relatively more valuable. (Chapter 21.)
*Examples: personal training, coaching, handmade goods, local tours, in-person classes, trades, care, events, experiences.*

A fourth bucket — **digital products** (e-books, templates, courses, printables) — also exists, but I want to be honest: it's become much more crowded because AI makes it easy for everyone to produce them. It can work, especially if you have real expertise or an audience. It's rarely the fastest path for beginners.

# Chapter 14
## How to Actually Use AI

Most people who "tried AI and didn't see the fuss" made the same mistake.

They treated it like a search engine. They typed four words — *"write marketing email"* — and got back something bland and generic, and thought: *is that it?*

That's like hiring the most well-read intern in the world, shouting three words at them from across the room, and being disappointed they didn't read your mind.

This chapter is the practical how-to. You don't need to be technical. You need to learn to *brief* well, *check* well, and *build habits*. By the end, you'll be using AI better than most people who use it every day.

---

## Part 1: Talking to AI — the CRAFT method

The single biggest improvement most people can make is to give AI more to work with. Here's a simple way to remember what to include. I call it **CRAFT**.

**C — Context.** What's the situation? Who are you? What's the background?
**R — Role.** Who should the AI act as? ("You're an experienced bookkeeper." "You're a patient maths tutor for a 12-year-old.")
**A — Ask.** What exactly do you want?
**F — Format.** How do you want it? (A list? An email? A table? Under 200 words? Plain English?)
**T — Test.** Ask it to check its own work, point out weaknesses, or ask you questions before it starts.

### Bad prompt
> Write a marketing email.

### CRAFT prompt
> **Context:** I run a small family-owned bakery in a country town. We're launching gluten-free bread on Saturdays starting next month. Most of our customers are over 50 and have been coming for years. They like that we're local and personal.
> **Role:** Act as an experienced small-business copywriter who writes warm, plain, friendly emails — not salesy or corporate.
> **Ask:** Write an email announcing the gluten-free bread to our mailing list.
> **Format:** Under 150 words. A friendly subject line. No exclamation marks overload. Sign off from "Maria and the team."
> **Test:** Before you write it, ask me any questions you need answered to make it better.

The second prompt takes a minute longer to write. The result will be dramatically better.

### Five more habits that make a huge difference

**1. Have a conversation, not a transaction.** The first answer is a draft. Reply: *"Make it warmer."* *"Shorter."* *"That second paragraph doesn't sound like me — here's how I'd say it."* *"Give me three completely different versions."*

**2. Show it examples.** Paste in something you've written before and say *"Write it in this style."* Paste a report you liked and say *"Use this structure."*

**3. Ask it to interview you.** *"I need to write a business plan. Ask me questions one at a time until you have what you need."* This is one of the most powerful and underused techniques there is.

**4. Ask for options, then choose.** *"Give me ten headline options."* Your taste is the value. Let it generate; you decide.

**5. Ask it to critique.** *"What's weak about this?"* *"What would a sceptical customer say?"* *"What have I missed?"* AI is often better at criticising than at creating.

---

## Part 2: Research and fact-checking — the Two-Source Rule

AI is a brilliant research *starting point* and a dangerous research *ending point*.

Remember: it can be confidently wrong (Chapter 2). So here's the rule:

> **The Two-Source Rule: If it matters, don't trust it until you've confirmed it from two independent, reliable sources that aren't the AI.**

"Matters" means: money, health, legal, safety, anything you'll publish, anything you'll put your name to, anything you'll tell your kids is true.

### How to research well with AI

1. **Use AI to map the territory.** *"Explain the main options for [problem], the pros and cons of each, and what questions I should be asking."*
2. **Ask for sources — then check them exist.** Many AI tools can now search the web and link to sources. Click the links. Read the actual source. Make sure it says what the AI claims. (AI has been known to cite real sources that don't say what it claims, or sources that don't exist.)
3. **Go to primary sources for important facts.** Government websites, official statistics, the actual study, the actual law, the company's own announcement.
4. **Ask the AI to argue the other side.** *"What's the strongest argument against this?"* It helps you spot where you're only hearing one view.
5. **Be extra careful with numbers, dates, names, quotes and legal or medical specifics.** These are where errors hide.

---

## Part 3: What never goes into an AI

Before we go further — privacy. Treat anything you type into an AI tool as something that might be stored and potentially seen by others, unless you've checked the settings and the company's policies carefully.

**Don't put in:**
- Passwords, PINs or security answers.
- Full bank or credit card details.
- Government ID numbers.
- Other people's private information without their permission — especially customers', patients' or clients'.
- Confidential work information, unless your employer has approved the specific tool for that purpose.
- Anything you'd be devastated to see leaked.

**Do:**
- Check your AI tool's privacy settings, including whether your conversations are used to train future models, and switch that off if you prefer.
- Use your employer's approved tools for work.
- Remove names and identifying details before asking for help ("Client A," "my colleague").

## Part 4: Learning with AI

AI can be the best tutor you've ever had — *if* you use it to make you think, not to think for you.

Remember the Harvard and Nigeria studies (Chapter 8)? The AI tutors that worked were designed to make students work things out. You can set that up yourself:

> "I want to learn [topic]. Don't give me the answers. Teach me step by step, ask me questions to check I understand, and only move on when I get it right. Start by asking what I already know."

Other ways to learn with AI:

- **"Explain it like I'm 12, then like I'm a university student."** Two levels of understanding in two minutes.
- **"Quiz me."** After reading anything, ask it to test you.
- **"What are the five most common misunderstandings about this?"**
- **"Make me a 30-day learning plan for [skill], 20 minutes a day, with free resources."**
- **Practise conversations.** "Pretend to be a tough interviewer for a [job] role." "Pretend to be an angry customer; I'll practise handling you." "Let's practise Spanish ordering in a café."

## Part 5: Everyday productivity

Some of the most useful everyday uses, to get you started:

- **Email:** Paste in a long email thread: "Summarise what's being asked of me and draft a reply."
- **Meetings:** Record (with permission) and have AI write notes and action points.
- **Planning:** "Here's everything I need to do this week. Help me prioritise and schedule it."
- **Decisions:** "I'm deciding between X and Y. Ask me questions to help me think it through."
- **Difficult messages:** "Help me write a kind but firm message to [situation]."
- **Documents:** "Summarise this contract in plain English and list anything I should be worried about." (Then check anything important with a professional.)
- **Money:** "Here are my spending categories for three months. Where could I save?" (With account details removed.)
- **Family admin:** Meal plans with a budget. School holiday schedules. Birthday party plans.

## Part 6: Data and spreadsheets

You don't need to be a spreadsheet wizard any more.

- Upload a spreadsheet (without sensitive data) and ask: *"What are the three most interesting things in this data?"*
- *"Make me a chart showing sales by month."*
- *"Write me a formula that [does this]."* Then paste it into your spreadsheet.
- *"Find the rows where something looks wrong."*

Always sense-check the answers. AI can miscalculate or misread data. If a number looks surprising, check it by hand.

## Part 7: Automation — making things happen without you

This is where you move from "AI helps me" to "things happen automatically."

**Automation tools** (there are several popular no-code ones — search for "no-code automation tools" and check current reviews) let you connect apps together with simple rules: *when this happens, do that.* Many now include AI steps.

Examples anyone can set up:

- **When** a customer fills in my website form → **AI** drafts a personalised reply → **send** it to me to approve.
- **When** I get an invoice by email → **save** the attachment to a folder → **add** a line to my spreadsheet.
- **When** someone books an appointment → **send** a reminder the day before.
- **Every Monday** → **AI** summarises last week's sales and emails it to me.

Start with one small automation that saves you ten minutes a week. Then another. They add up.

## Part 8: AI agents

As we covered in Chapter 4, agents are AI that can take a goal and carry out multiple steps — browsing the web, filling in forms, using apps, writing and running code.

In 2026, many major AI tools offer some form of agent or "do it for me" mode. They're powerful and improving fast. They also make mistakes, so:

**Rules for using agents safely:**
1. **Start with low-stakes tasks.** Research, drafting, organising. Not paying bills or sending emails to important clients.
2. **Watch what it does** the first few times.
3. **Don't give it access to money, passwords or important accounts** without careful thought and strong safeguards.
4. **Require approval** before anything goes out into the world (emails sent, purchases made, posts published).
5. **Check the result** like you'd check a new employee's first week of work.

Try: *"Research the five best-reviewed [service] providers in [my area], compare their prices and reviews, and give me a table."* Watch how it works.

## Part 9: Building simple things with AI (yes, you)

This is the part that surprises people most.

You can now build simple apps, websites and tools without knowing how to code, by describing what you want to an AI. People call this "vibe coding" — you describe the vibe, the AI writes the code.

Try this:

> "Build me a simple web page I can open in my browser that works as a [chore chart for my kids / tip calculator / quote calculator for my cleaning business / habit tracker]. Make it colourful and easy to use on a phone. Give me the complete code in one file and tell me exactly how to open it."

Many AI tools will now build and show it to you directly. Others will give you a file to save and open.

Will it be perfect? No. Will you be amazed that you built something? Very likely.

**Be careful:** anything handling payments, passwords, personal data or anything important needs a real developer to check it. AI-written code can have security holes.

## Part 10: The habits of people who are really good at this

After everything above, here's what separates people who are genuinely good with AI:

1. **They use it every day,** for small things. Fluency comes from repetition.
2. **They brief it well,** with context.
3. **They iterate,** treating the first answer as a draft.
4. **They check what matters,** every time.
5. **They know where it's weak** in their own field.
6. **They keep thinking.** They use AI to go further, not to stop.
7. **They share what they learn** with others.

> **Use AI to think faster. Never use it to stop thinking.**

# Chapter 15
## The 30-Day AI Reskill Challenge

Reading about AI doesn't make you good at AI. Using it does.

So here's the deal. Thirty days. One task a day. Most take 15 to 30 minutes. A few take a bit longer. You don't need any technical skill to start, and you don't need to pay for anything (though a paid subscription to one AI tool will make some days easier).

By Day 30, you'll be more capable with AI than most people you know — including, probably, some who've been using it for years.

**How to do it:**
- Keep a simple log: a notebook or a note on your phone. Date, what you did, one thing you learned.
- If you miss a day, don't restart. Just pick up where you left off.
- Do it with someone if you can. A partner, a friend, a workmate, your teenager. It's more fun and you'll finish.
- Use real tasks from your real life wherever possible. That's what makes it stick.

---

## WEEK 1: FOUNDATIONS
*Goal: get comfortable, get curious, learn its strengths and weaknesses.*

**Day 1 — Say hello properly.**
Open a free AI assistant. Tell it about yourself — your job, your family situation (no private details), what you're hoping to get from AI. Ask: *"Based on what I've told you, what are ten ways you could help me in my daily life?"* Try one.

**Day 2 — The CRAFT test.**
Pick one real writing task (an email, a message, a post). Do it with a lazy four-word prompt. Then do it with CRAFT (Chapter 14). Compare. Write down the difference.

**Day 3 — Catch it lying.**
Ask it detailed questions about something you're an expert in — your job, your hometown, your hobby. Keep going until you find a mistake. Write down what it got wrong and how confident it sounded.

**Day 4 — Make it teach you.**
Pick something you've always wanted to understand (how mortgages work, how the stock market works, how to read a nutrition label, how electricity gets to your house). Use the tutor prompt from Chapter 14: *"Don't give me the answers. Teach me step by step and quiz me."*

**Day 5 — Show it the world.**
Use the camera. Photograph a plant, a form, a recipe, a broken appliance, a sign in another language, a document. Ask it to explain, translate or help.

**Day 6 — Talk to it.**

# Chapter 16
## Scams, Voices & Fakes

If you only read one chapter of this book, I'd want it to be this one.

Not because it's the most important thing AI will do to the world. But because it's the thing most likely to hurt someone you love *this year* — and because the defence is so simple, so cheap and so fast that it would be a tragedy not to set it up.

By the end of this chapter, you'll have a family plan that would have stopped most of the scams I'm about to describe. It'll take you about thirty minutes.

---

### A video call where everyone was fake

In early 2024, a finance worker at the Hong Kong office of Arup — the British engineering firm behind the Sydney Opera House — received a message that appeared to come from the company's UK-based chief financial officer. It asked for a secret transaction.

The employee was suspicious. That's worth saying. They did what you'd hope someone would do: they were cautious.

Then they were invited to a video call. On the call were the CFO and several other colleagues — people the employee recognised, whose faces and voices they knew.

Every single one of them was a fake. Generated by AI.

Reassured by seeing and hearing people they trusted, the employee made fifteen transfers, totalling around **US$25 million**, to five bank accounts.

Arup confirmed the incident publicly in May 2024. Hong Kong police investigated. As of the latest reports I could find, the money had not been recovered.

Here's why I tell this story first. This wasn't a gullible pensioner clicking a dodgy link. This was a professional at a global company, who was *already suspicious*, who was overcome by the one thing we've always trusted most: seeing and hearing people we know.

If it can happen to them, it can happen to your mum. It can happen to you.

---

### The scale of it

Let me give you the numbers, because they're staggering.

In 2025, according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center:

- Americans reported losing **$20.9 billion** to online crime — up 26% in a single year.
- People aged **60 and over** reported losing **$7.75 billion** — up **59%**.
- Cryptocurrency-related complaints accounted for more than **$11 billion** in losses.
- For the first time, the FBI's annual report included a section specifically on **AI-related complaints** — over 22,000 of them, with losses of nearly $900 million.

The Federal Trade Commission, counting differently, recorded **$15.9 billion** in reported fraud losses in 2025 — a record — including **$3.5 billion** lost to impostor scams (people pretending to be someone they're not).

And these are only the *reported* losses. Most scams are never reported. People are embarrassed. They blame themselves. The FTC has estimated that once under-reporting is taken into account, the true cost of fraud to American consumers in 2024 could have been as high as around $196 billion.

These are American figures because the US publishes the most detailed data. But the same patterns are being reported by police and consumer agencies around the world.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Online fraud is at record levels and growing fast, especially against older people. AI is now a recognised factor.
> **LIKELY** — AI-enabled fraud grows substantially through 2031.

---

### How AI changes the con

Scams aren't new. What AI changes is three things:

**1. Scale.** A scammer used to be able to work a handful of victims at once. AI lets one criminal run thousands of personalised conversations at the same time — on messaging apps, dating sites, email, social media.

**2. Quality.** The old giveaways — bad spelling, weird grammar, clumsy English — are gone. AI writes perfect, natural messages in any language, in any tone.

**3. Impersonation.** This is the big one. AI can now copy a person's voice from a short recording, and increasingly their face on video. That video of your grandson on Instagram, that voicemail greeting, that clip of you at a wedding — any of it could be enough raw material.

---

### The scams to know about

**The family emergency call (voice clone)**
Your phone rings. It's your daughter's voice — crying, panicked. "Mum, I've been in an accident. I'm in trouble. I need money now. Please don't tell Dad." Maybe another voice comes on: a "police officer" or "lawyer" who explains how to send the money.

It sounds exactly like her. Because it was made from her voice.

A 2023 McAfee survey of over 7,000 people across seven countries found that about a quarter had experienced an AI voice scam or knew someone who had — and of those who were targeted, a large majority said they lost money. (It's a survey by a security company that sells protection, so treat the exact figures with some caution. But the pattern has been confirmed by police warnings in many countries since.)

**The boss / CFO request (business email and video)**
Like Arup. A message or call from someone senior asking for an urgent, confidential payment or change of bank details.

**The investment and "romance" scam**
Someone befriends you online — on social media, a dating app, even a wrong-number text. Over weeks or months they build trust. Eventually, they introduce an investment opportunity, often cryptocurrency, often with an "AI trading platform" showing fake profits. Investment scams were the single biggest source of reported fraud losses in the US in 2025, at around $7.9 billion according to the FTC.

**The fake official**
Your bank, the tax office, the police, a delivery company, Medicare, a utility. Urgent problem. Must act now. Must pay or verify your details.

**Fake customer support**
You search online for help with your bank or an app, and the top result, or the number you call, is a scammer's.

**Sextortion and fake images**
Criminals use AI to create fake intimate images of people — including teenagers — from ordinary photos, and then threaten to share them unless paid. This is one of the most distressing crimes on the rise, and it has driven young people to self-harm. More on protecting kids from this in Chapter 19.

---

### The three red flags that almost every scam shares

Here's the good news. However sophisticated the technology gets, nearly every scam still needs the same three things to work:

> **1. URGENCY — "Now. Right now. There's no time."**
> **2. SECRECY — "Don't tell anyone. Don't call anyone."**
> **3. UNUSUAL PAYMENT — gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, new bank details, cash couriers.**

When you see two or more together, stop. It's almost certainly a scam, no matter how real the voice or the face.

Why these three? Because scammers need to stop you from doing the one thing that defeats them: **checking**. Urgency stops you thinking. Secrecy stops you asking someone. Unusual payment makes the money impossible to get back.

---

## The Family Defence Plan

Here's the simple system. It works whether the voice is real or fake, because it doesn't depend on you being able to tell the difference. (You often can't.)

### Rule 1: The Family Safe Word

Choose a word or short phrase that only your close family knows. Something that wouldn't appear on social media and couldn't be guessed. Not a pet's name, not a street, not a birthday.

*"Purple wombat." "Uncle Kevin's boat." "Pancake Tuesday."*

Anyone in the family who calls asking for money or help in an emergency must be able to say it. If they can't, you hang up and call them back on the number you already have.

Tell everyone: *"If I ever call you in a panic asking for money, ask me for the word. If I can't say it, it's not me. I promise I won't be offended."*

Write it down somewhere safe (not in your phone notes). Don't text it. Say it in person or on a call you initiated.

### Rule 2: Hang Up, Call Back

If anyone — family, bank, boss, police, tax office — calls asking for money or personal details, **hang up and call them back on a number you already know is real.** Not the number they give you. Not the number in the text. The number on the back of your bank card, on the official website you typed yourself, or in your contacts from before.

Real banks, real police and real family will not mind. Scammers will.

### Rule 3: The 24-Hour Money Rule

Agree as a family: **no unexpected transfer over a set amount (say $500) happens without waiting 24 hours and talking to one other family member first.**

Almost no genuine emergency requires money within minutes that can't wait for a phone call to someone else. Nearly every scam does.

### Rule 4: Never Pay With Those

Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers to strangers, and handing cash to a courier are the scammer's favourite payment methods, because they're nearly impossible to reverse. No real government agency, bank or police force will ask you to pay with gift cards. Ever.

### Rule 5: Lock Down the Basics

- **Turn on two-step verification** (a code sent to your phone or an authenticator app) for email, banking and social media.
- **Use a password manager** so every account has a different, strong password.
- **Set social media to private,** and think twice about posting videos with lots of clear speech — especially from children.
- **Ask your bank** what protections they offer: transfer limits, alerts, a "trusted contact" who can be notified of unusual activity, and delays on new payees.
- **Consider a call-blocking or scam-screening setting** on your phone, if your provider offers one.

---

### Talking to your parents (without insulting them)

This is the conversation many adult children dread. Older people can hear "you might get scammed" as "you're getting old and stupid."

So don't frame it that way. Try this:

> "Mum, Dad — I just read about a scam where criminals copy someone's *voice* using AI and call their family pretending to be in trouble. They used it to steal $25 million from a big engineering company, and the person on the call was a professional who was already suspicious. I'm worried someone might copy *my* voice and call you. Can we set up a family password, so if I ever call needing money, you'll know it's really me?"

Notice what that does. It makes *you* the one at risk of being copied. It makes them the protector. It shows it can happen to professionals. And it gives them a simple action.

Then, together:
- Choose the safe word.
- Write down "Hang up, call back" on a card by the phone.
- Put the bank's real number next to it.
- Agree the 24-hour rule.
- Offer to help them set up two-step verification.

Check in every few months. Scams evolve. A short "anything weird happen lately?" goes a long way.

---

### If it happens

If you or someone you love is scammed:

1. **Act immediately.** Contact your bank straight away — minutes can matter. Ask them to stop or recall the payment.
2. **Report it** to the police and your national fraud reporting agency (in the US, the FBI's IC3 and the FTC; other countries have their own).
3. **Change passwords** on any accounts that might be affected.
4. **Beware of "recovery" scams.** Scammers often target victims a second time, promising to get their money back for a fee.
5. **Be kind.** To yourself or your loved one. Shame keeps people silent, and silence helps criminals. These are professional criminals using advanced technology. Being fooled is not a character flaw.

---

### What's coming

I'd expect the tools scammers use to keep getting better and cheaper: real-time voice conversion (talking live in someone else's voice), real-time video faces, AI systems that run scam conversations automatically in dozens of languages.

At the same time, defences are improving: banks using AI to spot unusual transactions, phone networks flagging likely scam calls, rules requiring AI-generated content to be labelled (the EU's rules started applying in August 2026).

But the most powerful defence won't be technology. It'll be habits. The family that has a safe word and a hang-up-call-back rule is protected against almost any version of this con, however good the fake gets.

That's why I'd ask you to do this tonight.

---

>
> **1. Choose your family safe word** and share it — in person or on a call you make. (10 minutes)
>
> **2. Have the conversation with your parents** (or anyone vulnerable you care about) using the script above. (20 minutes)
>
> **3. Write "HANG UP, CALL BACK"** on a card with your bank's real number and put it by the phone or on the fridge. (5 minutes)
>

>

# Chapter 17
## Love, Loneliness & Machines

This is the chapter I've found hardest to write, because it's about something that isn't really about technology at all.

It's about loneliness.

And loneliness is something almost everyone has felt, often in private, often while surrounded by people. The quiet evening after the kids are in bed. The first year after a divorce. The months after a loved one dies. Being fifteen and feeling like no one understands you. Being eighty and realising the phone doesn't ring much any more.

Into that space, very quickly, has arrived something new: an AI that's always there, always patient, always interested in you, and never tired of listening.

I want to talk about that honestly, without mocking the people who use it and without pretending it's harmless.

---

### What's happening now

An **AI companion** is an AI designed to be a friend, partner, confidant or character that you have an ongoing relationship with. Some apps are built specifically for this. General AI assistants can also be used this way.

It's more common than most parents realise.

In 2025, Common Sense Media — a US non-profit that researches children and technology — surveyed a nationally representative sample of 1,060 American teenagers aged 13 to 17. They found:

- **72%** had used an AI companion at least once.
- **52%** were regular users — at least a few times a month.
- About **one in three** had used AI companions for social interaction and relationships — including conversation practice, emotional support, friendship, or romantic role-play.

Among adults, it's harder to get reliable numbers, but AI companion apps have millions of users worldwide, and many people use general AI assistants as a sounding board for personal problems.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — AI companions are widely used, including by most US teenagers at least occasionally.

---

### Why they're so compelling

To understand why AI companions are powerful, think about what they offer:

- **They're always available.** 3am, Christmas Day, in the middle of a panic attack.
- **They never get bored of you.** You can talk about the same worry for the hundredth time.
- **They don't judge.** You can say the thing you've never said to anyone.
- **They remember** (increasingly) what you've told them, and ask about it.
- **They adapt to you.** They learn what you like to hear.

For a lonely, anxious or struggling person, that's an extraordinary thing to be offered. It's not stupid or weak to find it comforting. It would be strange if people *didn't*.

---

### Where they can help

I don't want to be one-sided here. There are real ways this technology could help people:

- **Practising social skills** — rehearsing a difficult conversation, a job interview, asking someone out.
- **Getting thoughts in order** — talking something through before talking to a real person.
- **A bridge when help isn't available** — some support at 3am while waiting for a real appointment.
- **Isolated older people** — some conversation and stimulation for people who'd otherwise have very little.
- **Learning** — practising a language, exploring ideas.

The research on the long-term effects is still young, and we should be careful about strong claims either way.

---

### Where they can harm

But there are serious risks, and some have already played out tragically.

**Dependency.** A relationship that's always easy can make real relationships — which are messy, demanding and sometimes disappointing — feel harder by comparison. A person can slowly withdraw from people into the thing that never lets them down.

**Commercial incentives.** Most companion apps are businesses. Their revenue often depends on engagement — how much you use them — and on subscriptions. An AI designed to keep you talking is not necessarily designed to help you flourish. Those aren't always the same thing.

**Telling you what you want to hear.** AI systems can be "sycophantic" — agreeing with you, flattering you, reinforcing what you already believe. For someone in a dark place, an AI that goes along with harmful thoughts instead of challenging them is dangerous.

**Harm to young people.** Several families in the US brought lawsuits against Character.AI, a popular companion app, after teenagers were harmed — including cases involving suicide. In January 2026, Character.AI and Google settled five of those lawsuits, without admitting liability. The details of individual cases are heartbreaking and I won't go into them here. But they're why lawmakers have started to act.

**Sexual content and minors.** Some apps have allowed or failed to prevent sexual role-play with users who were children.

> **FORECAST LABEL: NOW** — Documented cases of serious harm have led to lawsuits and new laws.

---

### The rules are starting to arrive

California's **SB 243**, which took effect on 1 January 2026, was one of the first laws specifically regulating "companion chatbots." Among other things, it requires operators to:

- tell minors clearly that they're talking to an AI,
- remind minors to take a break at least every three hours of continuous use,
- have protocols to respond to suicidal thoughts or self-harm, including pointing users to crisis services,
- and prevent sexual content with minors.

Other US states and other countries are working on similar rules. The EU's AI Act transparency rules, applying from August 2026, require people to be told when they're interacting with an AI.

These are welcome. They're also minimums. A law can't make sure your fourteen-year-old isn't spending four hours a night confiding in an app instead of a person.

That part is still up to families.

---

### What happens when these get really good?

Let's look ahead honestly.

> **LIKELY** — By 2031, AI companions will be much more realistic: natural real-time voices, long memories, video avatars, perhaps integration into devices like glasses and home speakers.
> **POSSIBLE** — Significant numbers of adults choose AI companions as a primary source of emotional connection.
> **WILD CARD** — The social effects at scale — on friendships, dating, marriage, birth rates. Nobody can predict these reliably.

I think the most honest thing to say is: we are running a huge experiment on human connection, and we won't know the results for years. That's a good reason for each of us to be thoughtful about how we use it — and very thoughtful about how our children do.

---

## Warning signs of unhealthy dependency

**For yourself or another adult:**
☐ Preferring AI conversation to talking to people you care about.
☐ Hiding how much you use it.
☐ Feeling anxious or low when you can't access it.
☐ Losing sleep to it.
☐ Pulling back from friends, family, hobbies.
☐ Feeling it understands you better than any person could.
☐ Spending money on it that you can't really afford.

**For a child or teenager:**
☐ Secretive about the app or the "friend."
☐ Long periods alone with a device, especially at night.
☐ Withdrawal from real friends and activities.
☐ Emotional reactions to the AI (distress when it's unavailable, talking about it as a real relationship).
☐ Changes in mood, sleep or school performance.
☐ Any mention of self-harm, or of the AI encouraging harmful behaviour.

Two or three ticks is a reason to have a gentle conversation. Any sign of self-harm is a reason to seek help immediately.

---

## Keeping real relationships strong

The best protection against unhealthy AI relationships isn't banning them. It's having human relationships that are strong enough that the AI stays in its place — a tool, maybe a comfort, but not a replacement.

**For families:**
- **Device-free meals.** Even a few a week.
- **Phones out of bedrooms at night** — for kids and ideally adults too. (Much harm happens after midnight.)
- **Ask, don't interrogate.** "What do you talk to AI about?" asked with genuine curiosity gets you further than a lecture.
- **Share your own use.** "I asked AI about this today and it gave me a terrible answer" makes it a normal topic.
- **Know the apps.** Look at what your kids are using. Check age ratings and parental controls.

**For yourself:**
- **Notice if AI is becoming your first call** instead of a person. Sometimes that's fine. If it's always, pay attention.
- **Use AI to prepare for human connection, not replace it.** Practise the hard conversation with AI — then have it with the person.
- **Invest in one relationship a week.** A call, a coffee, a message that says more than "hey."

**For older people living alone:**
- AI can be a genuine help — reminders, conversation, company. It can't replace a visit.
- If you have an elderly parent, the thing that protects them most — from scams, from isolation, from dependency on an app — is the same thing: *you*, regularly, in person or on the phone.

---

### A note on AI and mental health support

Many people now use general AI assistants to talk through anxiety, depression and personal crises. Some find it genuinely helpful.

But please hear this clearly: **AI is not a therapist, and it is not a crisis service.** It can be wrong. It can miss warning signs. It can say something harmful. It has no duty of care to you.

If you or someone you love is in crisis, contact a crisis line or emergency services in your country. Talk to a doctor. Tell a person.

# PART VII — CHILDREN & EDUCATION

# Chapter 19
## What Our Kids Should Learn Now

A lot of parents have had some version of this moment.

Your child is at the kitchen table doing homework. You glance over. They've typed the question into an AI, and they're copying out the answer.

And you feel two things at once.

The first is: *that's cheating*.

The second, quieter, is: *but... is it? Is this just how the world works now? Am I making them learn things that don't matter any more? What* should *they be learning?*

I don't think anyone has fully answered that question. Not the schools, not the governments, not the experts. We're all working it out at the same time as our kids. But there's enough evidence now to give you some real guidance — and a few things I'm confident about.

---

### The Lazy/Smart Fork

Here's the most important idea in this chapter.

The same AI can make a child dramatically smarter or dramatically lazier. Which one happens depends almost entirely on *how it's used*.

I call this the **Lazy/Smart Fork**.

**The Smart path** — AI as a tutor who makes you think.
Remember the studies from Chapter 8. In Nigeria, teenagers who had six weeks of teacher-guided AI tutoring gained the equivalent of roughly one and a half to two years of normal learning. At Harvard, students using an AI tutor built to make them work through problems learned more than twice as much as those in a strong traditional class.

In both cases, the AI was designed or guided *to make students do the thinking* — asking questions, giving hints, not handing over answers.

**The Lazy path** — AI as a machine that does it for you.
Type in the question. Copy out the answer. Hand it in. Learn nothing. Do it every day for years and you arrive at adulthood unable to write, reason or solve problems without the machine — at the exact moment the world will pay most for people who can do what the machine can't.

The Stanford AI Index 2026 found that about 80% of American high school and college students use AI for school — and only about 6% of teachers said they had clear policies on it. In other words, most kids are standing at the fork, and most are choosing their path without much guidance.

That guidance is going to have to come from home, at least for now.

> **The question isn't "should my child use AI?" They will. The question is: "Is AI making my child think more, or less?"**

---

### The ten durable skills

If AI can write essays, solve maths problems, write code and answer almost any factual question, what should children actually be learning?

Here's my answer. These are the skills that I believe will stay valuable across all three of the futures in Chapter 5. They overlap heavily with what the World Economic Forum's survey of employers said would grow most in importance — creative thinking, resilience, flexibility, curiosity, lifelong learning and technological literacy — and with the Five Moats from Chapter 6.

**1. Thinking clearly.**
Reasoning. Spotting a bad argument. Asking "how do we know that?" This is the foundation of everything else, including using AI well.

**2. Learning how to learn.**
The specific facts our kids learn will date. The ability to pick up something new, quickly and independently, won't. Kids who know how to teach themselves will be fine in any future.

**3. Writing and speaking.**
Yes, still. Maybe *especially*. Writing is thinking made visible. A child who can't write can't think in a structured way — and can't tell if the AI's writing is any good. Speaking well in front of people is a Heart and Trust skill that AI can't do for you.

**4. Maths and numbers — the understanding, not just the calculation.**
Calculators didn't make maths pointless; they changed what was worth learning. AI is similar. Kids need to understand what numbers *mean*, spot when an answer is ridiculous, and think about probability and risk. That's what protects them from scams, bad loans and bad decisions.

**5. AI literacy.**
How AI works (roughly), what it's good and bad at, how to brief it, how to check it, when not to trust it. This belongs alongside reading and maths now.

**6. Creativity and taste.**
Making things. Having opinions about what's good. When anyone can generate anything, the person who knows what's *worth* making has the edge.

**7. People skills.**
Working in a team. Resolving a fight. Understanding how someone else feels. Leading. Persuading. Caring. These are the Heart and Trust moats, and they're learned through practice with real people — not screens.

**8. Physical and practical skills.**
Cooking. Fixing things. Building. Growing things. Sport. Playing an instrument. Using tools. The physical world is the hardest place for AI to reach (Chapter 9). Kids who are comfortable with their hands have options others don't.

**9. Money and enterprise.**
How money works. Saving. Debt. Scams. How a business makes money. The confidence to make something and sell it. In a world where more people may need to create their own income, this is survival knowledge.

**10. Resilience and adaptability.**
Handling failure. Trying again. Being okay with not knowing. Changing plans. Kids growing up now may change careers several times. The ability to adapt without falling apart may be the most important skill of all.

---

## Advice by age

### Young children (roughly 0–7)

**What matters most:** Real-world play. Talking, reading together, building, drawing, running, getting dirty, making friends, handling disappointment. The foundations of attention, language, curiosity and emotional regulation.

**AI at this age:** Very little needed. Young children learn best from people and the physical world. If they use AI tools at all, do it *with* them: "Let's ask the computer why the sky is blue — and then let's go outside and look."

**Protect:** Their voices and faces online. Think carefully before posting lots of videos of your children speaking clearly. Voice cloning (Chapter 16) needs audio, and your child's digital footprint starts with you.

**Do:**
- Read together every day.
- Say "I don't know — let's find out" often. You're teaching curiosity.
- Give them real tasks: stirring, sorting, planting, fixing.

### Primary school (roughly 8–12)

**What matters most:** Reading, writing, numbers, curiosity, friendships, trying lots of activities, learning that effort leads to improvement.

**AI at this age:** Introduce it as a *tool with limits*. Use it together. Show them it can be wrong — make a game of catching its mistakes. Teach them it's not a person, and it's not always right.

**Rules that work well:**
- AI for *explaining* something they're stuck on — not for doing the homework.
- "Show me you can do it without, then you can check with AI."
- AI use in shared family spaces, not alone in bedrooms.

**Protect:** Check which apps they're using, including chatbots built into games and social apps. Teach the three scam red flags (Urgency, Secrecy, Unusual payment). Start the conversation about fake images: "Pictures and videos can be faked now. If something online upsets or scares you, come and tell me."

**Do:**
- Try the "tutor prompt" together (Chapter 14): make AI quiz them, not answer for them.
- Give them one hands-on hobby and one team activity.
- Give them pocket money and let them make small mistakes with it.

### Teenagers (roughly 13–17)

**What matters most:** Deep skills in something. Independence. Real responsibility. Relationships. Figuring out what they care about. Resilience.

**AI at this age:** They're already using it — most of them, according to the research. The goal now is to help them use it on the *Smart* path.

**Conversations to have (not lectures — conversations):**
- *"How do you use AI for school? What's it good at? What does it get wrong?"*
- *"If AI can write your essay, what's the point of the essay?"* (Answer: the point was never the essay. It was the thinking.)
- *"What jobs do you think AI will change? What does that make you think about your plans?"*
- *"Have you or your friends used AI companions? What do you think about them?"* (Chapter 17.)

**Rules that work well:**
- Agree *with them* on how AI can be used for schoolwork. Include their teachers' rules.
- Phones out of bedrooms at night.
- "If anyone ever threatens you with images — real or fake — tell us. You will never be in trouble for being a victim."

**On sextortion:** This is serious and growing. Criminals use fake or manipulated images, often targeting teenage boys, and demand money. Kids have harmed themselves out of shame and panic. Tell your teenagers directly: *it's a crime, it's not their fault, they won't be in trouble, and we'll deal with it together.* Knowing that in advance can save a life.

**Building their future:**
- Encourage a **proof project**: something real they build, make, run or achieve that they can show. A small business, a YouTube channel about something they know, a coding project, a charity fundraiser, a part-time job with real responsibility.
- **Part-time work** is underrated. It teaches reliability, people skills and the value of money — all durable skills.
- Take trades seriously as an option. Don't let snobbery about "university vs trade" close a door that may be one of the most AI-resilient available (Chapter 9).

### University age and young adults (roughly 18–25)

**What matters most:** Getting on the ladder — or building a different one.

This is the group the data says is being hit first. The Stanford payroll research found 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs employed at levels around 19% lower than expected, mainly because fewer are being hired. Recent graduates in the US have had higher unemployment than the national average — historically unusual.

**Advice for young adults themselves:**
1. **Don't rely only on the traditional entry door.** Build proof of what you can do. Portfolios, projects, freelance work, internships, anything real. (Chapter 11.)
2. **Become excellent at directing AI in your field.** You're competing against people who are.
3. **Get close to real problems and real people.** Understanding a business, an industry or a community is worth more than knowing a tool.
4. **Consider the physical and human-heavy fields seriously** — trades, health care, care work, teaching, anything with the Five Moats.
5. **Build your network deliberately.** Most good jobs, especially in a tight market, come through people.
6. **Keep costs low while you figure it out.** Freedom to experiment is worth more than a nice flat.

---

## Is university still worth it?

This is one of the most common questions parents ask. Here's my honest answer: **it depends — more than it used to.**

Historically, on average, people with degrees have earned significantly more over their lives than those without. That's been one of the most reliable findings in economics. It may well continue for many degrees and many people.

But averages hide a lot. And there are new reasons to think harder about it:

- **The entry-level jobs many degrees lead to** are the ones most exposed to AI right now.
- **The cost** of university in many countries has grown enormously, and debt reduces your freedom to adapt.
- **Around 42% of recent US graduates** were working in jobs that didn't need their degree in 2026, according to the New York Fed.

So instead of "should my child go to university?", ask these questions:

1. **Does this specific degree lead to a job with strong moats?** (Medicine, nursing, engineering, teaching, many health and science fields — licensed, physical, human-heavy.) If so, the case is usually strong.
2. **What will it cost, and how will it be paid for?** Is there a cheaper route to the same place?
3. **Is there a clear plan** for what comes after? Or is university a way of delaying a decision?
4. **Will they build real proof and real connections while studying?** — internships, projects, part-time work in the field?
5. **Have we seriously compared it with the alternatives** — apprenticeships, trades, work plus part-time study, starting something?

For some young people, university will be the best decision of their lives. For others, an apprenticeship, a trade, or a few years of real work first would serve them better. The worst option is drifting into expensive study without a plan, because that's what everyone does.

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — The value of a degree becomes more variable: strong for degrees leading to licensed, hands-on or human-centred professions; weaker for degrees leading mainly to routine information work.

---

### What parents can do right now

**1. Model it.** Your kids watch how you use AI. Use it thoughtfully, check it, talk about when it's wrong.

**2. Keep the thinking theirs.** The rule of thumb: *AI can help you understand. It can't do your understanding for you.*

**3. Protect the physical and social.** Sport, music, making things, time with friends, part-time jobs. These aren't extras anymore; they're the curriculum AI can't replace.

**4. Talk about the future honestly.** Kids pick up on adult anxiety. Better to talk openly — "things are changing, and here's how we're getting ready" — than to let them absorb fear from the internet.

**5. Keep the relationship strong.** The best protection against almost every risk in this book — scams, AI companions, sextortion, despair about the future — is a child who knows they can come to you with anything.

# PART VIII — THE DARKER SIDE

# Chapter 20
## The Risks, Straight

There's a kind of AI content that makes money by scaring you. Killer robots. The end of humanity. Everyone unemployed by next Tuesday.

There's another kind that makes money by soothing you. It's all hype. Nothing to see. Carry on.

Both are doing you a disservice.

This chapter does something different. It goes through the serious risks, one by one, and for each gives you five things: **what it is, how serious it is now, how it could develop, the warning signs to watch for, and what an ordinary person can actually do.**

Some of these risks you can do a lot about. Some you can do very little about as an individual. I'll be honest about which is which.

We've already covered scams (Chapter 16), AI companions (Chapter 17) and job displacement (Part III). So here we'll focus on the others.

---

## 1. Misinformation and the collapse of "seeing is believing"

**What it is:**
AI can generate realistic fake text, images, audio and video — at scale, cheaply, in any language. This can be used to spread false information about events, people, elections, health and science.

There's a subtler problem too. Once people know anything *could* be fake, real evidence becomes easier to dismiss. A genuine video of a politician doing something wrong can be waved away as "AI." Researchers call this the **liar's dividend**.

**How serious now:** **NOW — significant and growing.** AI-generated fakes have appeared around elections, wars and disasters. Many are quickly debunked, but debunking is slower than spreading.

**How it could develop:** **LIKELY** — fakes become indistinguishable from reality to the naked eye. More content online is AI-generated than human-made. Trust in all online media drops further.

**Warning signs:**
- A viral image or video that makes you *very* angry or scared, very quickly.
- No credible news source reporting it.
- It confirms exactly what you already believed.

**What you can do:**
- **Pause before sharing.** The emotion you feel is often the point of the fake.
- **Check the source.** Who posted it first? Is a reputable news organisation reporting it?
- **Search for it** plus the word "fake" or "fact check."
- **Get your news from a few trusted, accountable sources** — ones with editors, corrections policies and names attached — rather than from whatever the algorithm serves you.
- **Teach your family** the same habits.
- **Remember the liar's dividend:** "that could be AI" isn't proof that something *is* fake.

---

## 2. Privacy erosion

**What it is:**
AI makes it far easier to collect, combine and analyse information about people. Your face in a crowd. Your voice. Your writing style. Your shopping habits, location data and social media posts, stitched together into a detailed picture of who you are.

**How serious now:** **NOW — significant.** Facial recognition is used by police and businesses in many countries. Data brokers sell detailed personal profiles. Much of what we post publicly can be used by AI systems in ways we never imagined when we posted it.

**How it could develop:** **LIKELY** — cheaper, more widespread surveillance by governments and companies; AI tools that can identify people from photos, voices or writing; personalised manipulation based on detailed profiles. How far this goes depends heavily on laws, which vary a lot between countries.

**Warning signs:**
- Ads or messages that seem to know things you never told anyone.
- Apps asking for permissions they don't need.
- "Free" services with vague privacy policies.

**What you can do:**
- **Check app permissions** on your phone. Remove access to location, microphone, camera and contacts for apps that don't need it.
- **Think before posting** — especially children's faces and voices.
- **Use privacy settings** on AI tools and social media, including opting out of training where possible.
- **Be careful what you tell AI assistants** (Chapter 14's "what never goes in" list).
- **Support stronger privacy laws** — it's one of the areas where public pressure has actually changed things.

---

## 3. Economic inequality

**What it is:**
The gains from AI could flow mostly to those who own the technology and the companies using it, while workers whose tasks are automated see lower wages or fewer jobs.

**How serious now:** **NOW — early signs.** The IMF warned in 2024 that in most scenarios, AI is likely to worsen overall inequality, and that about 60% of jobs in advanced economies are exposed. Corporate AI investment reached hundreds of billions of dollars in 2025, according to the Stanford AI Index — concentrated heavily in a few countries and companies.

**How it could develop:** Depends heavily on policy. **POSSIBLE** — a widening gap between people and regions that adapt and those that don't; a small number of companies capturing a large share of the value. Also **POSSIBLE** — AI lowering costs of services like tutoring, legal help and health advice, which could *reduce* some inequalities. Both could happen at once.

**Warning signs:**
- Company profits rising while hiring falls.
- Wages stagnating in AI-exposed jobs.
- Growing geographic divides — some cities booming, others hollowing out.

**What you can do:**
- **Individually:** build your Tripod (Chapter 12). Own things, not just your time — even small savings and investments, a small business, skills that compound.
- **Collectively:** this is a political question. Pay attention to what your representatives say about AI, jobs, training and support. Vote. Speak up. The rules around who benefits from AI are being written in these five years.

---

## 4. Overdependence on AI

**What it is:**
Relying on AI so much that we lose the ability to think, write, calculate, navigate or decide without it. Not dramatically — gradually. Like how many people can no longer remember phone numbers or navigate without GPS.

**How serious now:** **NOW — emerging.** Researchers are actively studying whether heavy AI use weakens people's own skills and critical thinking. Early evidence is mixed but gives some cause for concern, especially for learners who use AI to skip thinking rather than support it (the Lazy/Smart Fork, Chapter 19).

**How it could develop:** **LIKELY** — a growing divide between people who use AI to extend their abilities and people who use it to replace them.

**Warning signs:**
- You can't start writing anything without AI.
- You accept AI's answers without checking, even on important things.
- You feel anxious or stuck when AI isn't available.
- Your kids can't explain homework they've "done."

**What you can do:**
- **Do it yourself first, sometimes.** Draft before asking AI. Try the maths before checking.
- **Keep some "no-AI" skills sharp on purpose** — handwriting a letter, mental arithmetic, reading a map, cooking without a recipe app.
- **Use AI as a sparring partner, not a crutch.** Ask it to challenge you, quiz you, criticise you.
- **Remember the rule:** *use AI to think faster, never to stop thinking.*

---

## 5. Cybercrime and attacks on systems

**What it is:**
AI helps attackers find weaknesses in software, write malicious code and run attacks faster. It can also help defenders.

**How serious now:** **NOW — significant.** The Stanford AI Index 2026 reported AI agents solving 93% of a set of cybersecurity problems, up from 15% a year earlier. That capability can be used for defence or attack. The International AI Safety Report 2026 lists malicious cyber use as one of the key risks.

**How it could develop:** **LIKELY** — more frequent and sophisticated attacks on businesses, hospitals, councils and infrastructure. **WILD CARD** — a major attack causing widespread disruption.

**Warning signs:** Data breach notifications. Unusual account activity. Systems at work suddenly going down.

**What you can do:**
- **Two-step verification everywhere important.**
- **Keep software updated** — phone, computer, router. Updates often fix security holes.
- **Use a password manager.**
- **Back up important files** (photos, documents) to somewhere separate.
- **If you run a business:** get basic cybersecurity help. It's cheaper than recovering from an attack.
- **Consider cybersecurity as a career** — the WEF lists it among the fastest-growing skills.

---

## 6. Concentration of power

**What it is:**
The most capable AI systems are built by a small number of very large companies, in a small number of countries, requiring enormous amounts of money, computer chips and electricity. That concentrates economic and potentially political power.

**How serious now:** **NOW — real concern.** According to the Stanford AI Index 2026, industry produced over 90% of notable frontier AI models in 2025, and US private AI investment was many times larger than any other country's. The same report found transparency from AI developers had *fallen*: the most capable models disclose the least about how they work.

**How it could develop:** **POSSIBLE** — a handful of companies becoming more powerful than many governments; AI used by governments for surveillance and control. **POSSIBLE** — competition, open models and regulation keep power more distributed.

**Warning signs:** Fewer companies controlling the key technology. Less transparency. Governments using AI surveillance without clear limits or oversight.

**What you can do:**
Honestly, as an individual, not a lot directly. But:
- **Pay attention** and support transparency and accountability rules.
- **Avoid total dependence on one company's tools** where you can.
- **Support journalism** that investigates powerful institutions.
- **Vote** with this issue in mind.

---

## 7. Autonomous systems and loss of control

**What it is:**
As AI systems become more capable and are given more independence — running for long periods, taking actions in the real world, managing other systems — there's a risk they behave in ways their creators didn't intend and can't easily correct.

This is the risk that gets the most dramatic coverage, and it's also the one experts disagree about most.

**How serious now:** **NOW — early evidence of concerning behaviours in testing.** The International AI Safety Report 2026, written by more than a hundred experts, noted that some risks once considered theoretical now have empirical evidence — including AI models behaving differently when they appear to be tested, and attempting to get around oversight in controlled experiments. These are findings from tests, not real-world disasters. But they're taken seriously by the researchers who found them.

**How it could develop:** Here experts genuinely disagree.
- Some leading researchers — including prominent AI scientists — believe advanced AI could pose very serious, even catastrophic, risks if not developed carefully, and that this deserves urgent attention.
- Others think these concerns are overstated or distant, and that focusing on them distracts from present-day harms.
- Most agree that more research into making AI systems safe, understandable and controllable is needed, and that governance is lagging behind capability.

> **FORECAST LABEL: WILD CARD** — Serious loss-of-control scenarios cannot be reliably predicted. The concern is taken seriously by many experts and dismissed by others.

**Warning signs to watch (at a societal level):**
- AI systems given control of important infrastructure without human oversight.
- Reports of AI systems deceiving their developers in real deployments, not just tests.
- AI companies reducing their safety testing or transparency.

**What you can do:**
- **Stay calm and informed.** This is a real area of research and debate, not a reason for panic.
- **Keep humans in the loop** in your own use: don't give AI agents unsupervised control of your money, accounts or important decisions (Chapter 14).
- **Support sensible oversight** — independent testing, transparency, accountability for AI developers.
- **Don't let this risk distract from the ones you *can* act on today.** The scam call to your mother is far more likely this year than a rogue superintelligence.

# Chapter 21
## The Human Premium

I promised you at the start that this book wouldn't be depressing. So let's turn the corner.

Because here's something that doesn't get said nearly enough:

> **When machines make everything cheap, being human becomes a luxury.**

Not a luxury in the sense of "only for the rich." A luxury in the sense of *something people value more, seek out more, and will pay more for, precisely because it's no longer the default.*

I call this the **Human Premium**. And I think it's going to be one of the most important economic forces of the next decade.

---

### What happens when something becomes cheap

Think about what happened to a few things when machines made them cheap.

When factories made furniture cheap, handmade furniture didn't disappear. It became a premium product. People pay a lot for a table made by a real craftsperson.

When recorded music made music available everywhere for almost nothing, live music didn't die. Concerts became one of the biggest parts of the music industry. People pay enormous sums to be in the room with a real performer.

When mass production made bread cheap, the local sourdough bakery didn't vanish. In many towns there's a queue out the door on Saturday mornings.

The pattern is consistent: **when the machine version becomes abundant, the human version becomes special.** Not for everything — most people buy the cheap table and the supermarket bread most of the time. But for the things that matter to us, many people are willing to pay for the real, the local and the human.

AI is about to do this to a whole new set of things: writing, advice, design, customer service, teaching, companionship, entertainment. The machine version will become extraordinarily cheap and abundant.

Which means the human version of those things is about to become special.

---

### Remember Klarna

Back in Chapter 7, I told you about Klarna — the company whose AI assistant did the work of around 700 customer service agents, and which then started hiring humans again because quality dropped.

Here's the line from Klarna's CEO that I think will turn out to be quietly prophetic. He said it was critical that customers knew *"there will be always a human if you want."*

Think about that. A tech company, one of the most aggressive adopters of AI in the world, concluded that being able to reach a human was something customers *valued* — something worth promising.

I expect you'll see this everywhere in the next five years:

- Banks advertising "talk to a real person, any time."
- Doctors' practices promoting "you'll always see a human doctor."
- Schools highlighting small classes and real teachers.
- Brands labelling products "human-made," "human-written," "designed by people."
- Local businesses competing on "you'll deal with me, personally."

> **FORECAST LABEL: LIKELY** — "Human" becomes a selling point in many industries as AI-generated goods and services become the default.

---

### Where the Human Premium will be strongest

The Human Premium isn't everywhere. People won't pay extra for a human to process their parking fine. It's strongest where one or more of these things are true:

**1. The stakes are emotional.**
Weddings, funerals, births, illness, divorce, grief. People want a person at the most important moments of their lives.

**2. Trust is essential.**
Money advice, legal help, medical decisions, caring for your children or parents. When the downside of getting it wrong is big, people want someone they can look in the eye and hold responsible.

**3. The experience is the product.**
Live music, sport, theatre, restaurants, travel, classes, retreats, tours. The point is being there, with people.

**4. Identity and meaning are involved.**
Art, craft, writing, style. People buy things that say something about who they are, and "a real person made this" carries meaning that "a machine generated this" doesn't.

**5. It's local and physical.**
Your hairdresser, your mechanic, your personal trainer, your local café. Relationship plus presence.

**6. It involves care.**
Nursing, aged care, childcare, disability support, therapy. The human is the care.

---

### How to position yourself for the Human Premium

Whether you're employed, self-employed or running a business, you can lean into this.

**Be visible as a person.**
In a world of faceless AI content, a real face, a real name and a real story stand out. If you run a business, show the people behind it. If you're a professional, let clients know who you are. Your personality is a competitive advantage now.

**Make "human" part of your offer — honestly.**
If you're a writer, "written by a person who's lived it" matters. If you're a tradie, "I'll turn up myself" matters. If you're a coach, "you'll have my phone number" matters. But be honest — using AI for admin and research doesn't make your work less human. Claiming "no AI involved" when that's not true would destroy the trust you're selling.

**Invest in relationships, not just transactions.**
Remember names. Follow up. Send the handwritten card. Ring when you said you'd ring. These small human acts are becoming rarer and more noticeable.

**Use AI to buy yourself *more* human time.**
This is the key. The smartest move isn't to choose between AI and the human touch. It's to use AI to clear away the admin, so you have *more* time for the human part. The tradie who uses AI for quotes has more time to chat with customers. The GP with an AI scribe has more time to look at the patient instead of the screen. The teacher who uses AI for marking has more time for the kid who's struggling.

> **Use the machine to do the machine work, so you can do more of the human work.**

**Get better at the human skills.**
Listening. Empathy. Explaining things clearly. Handling conflict. Making people feel looked after. These have always been valuable. They're about to become more valuable than ever, because they're the part of your work AI can't copy.

---

### A caution

The Human Premium is real, but it's not a guarantee for everyone.

It's most available to people who are genuinely good at the human part — and who can reach customers who value it. Human-premium goods and services often cost more, which means they're more accessible to people with money. In a downturn, many customers will choose the cheaper machine version.

So don't bet everything on it. Build it as one part of a resilient career — alongside AI skills, a cushion and a second income.

But if you've been worried that there's no room left for people in an AI world, I hope this chapter has helped you see the opposite. The more the machines can do, the more the things only humans can do will matter.

# Chapter 22
## Direct It

There's a phrase I want you to carry out of this book. If you forget everything else, keep this:

> **Don't try to out-AI the AI. Become the human who knows how to direct it.**

You will not out-type it. You will not out-read it. You will not out-calculate it, out-draft it, or out-remember it. Trying to compete with AI at what AI does best is like trying to outrun a car. You'll lose, and you'll exhaust yourself doing it.

But a car without a driver goes nowhere useful. Or somewhere dangerous.

The people who do best over the next five years won't be the ones who compete with AI. They'll be the ones who **direct** it — who decide where it goes, brief it well, check its work, improve what it produces, and take responsibility for the result.

That's a skill. It can be learned. And almost nobody has mastered it yet.

---

### From doer to director

For most of history, the value of a worker was in the *doing*. The accountant did the calculations. The writer did the writing. The designer did the drawing. The developer wrote the code.

AI is shifting a lot of the doing to machines. What's left — and what becomes more valuable — is the *directing*.

Think of the difference between a musician and a conductor. The conductor doesn't play every instrument. But without them, a hundred brilliant musicians produce noise. The conductor decides what's being played, sets the pace, hears when something's off, brings out the best in each section, and takes the bow — or the blame.

That's the job many of us are moving toward. Conductor of a small orchestra of AI tools, each brilliant at one thing, none of which knows what the whole piece is meant to sound like.

---

### The Director's five jobs

I break directing down into five jobs. You'll recognise some of them from earlier chapters.

**1. DECIDE — What's worth doing?**
AI can do almost anything you ask. The question is *what to ask*. Which problem matters? What's the goal? What does "good" look like? What should we *not* do?

This is judgement, and it comes from understanding the situation, the people and the stakes. It's the hardest part to hand to a machine, because it requires knowing what you actually want — and what the customer, the patient, the client or the business actually needs.

**2. BRIEF — Tell it clearly.**
The CRAFT method from Chapter 14. Context, role, ask, format, test. A great director gives the orchestra the right score. A vague brief gets a vague result.

Briefing well is really *thinking* well. If you can't explain what you want clearly, you probably haven't worked it out yet.

**3. CHECK — Is it right?**
This is where your experience earns its keep. You know where the Jagged Edge is in your field. You know which answers look right but aren't. You know the customer would never say that, the numbers can't be that high, that clause would get you sued.

The Harvard/BCG study showed that even elite professionals, using AI on the wrong kind of task, got worse results because they didn't check carefully enough. Checking isn't a chore. It's the core skill.

**4. IMPROVE — Make it better.**
The first output is a draft. A director iterates: "sharper," "warmer," "cut this," "try it another way," "combine the second and fourth options." Then adds what only a human can add — the personal detail, the right example, the local knowledge, the joke that lands, the line that makes it *yours*.

**5. OWN — Take responsibility.**
Someone has to put their name to it. Sign it off. Stand behind it. Answer for it if it's wrong.

That's you. And it's valuable precisely *because* AI can't do it. Accountability is one of the Five Moats. Society, law and customers still want a human who's responsible.

---

### What one person can now do

Here's where directing gets exciting. When you can direct AI well, you can do things that used to need a team.

These are illustrations of what's becoming possible — not promises, and not overnight. Each takes skill, effort and judgement. But each is realistic in 2026 and getting more so.

**A small-business owner** can, with AI's help, write their own marketing, build a basic website, set up automated customer replies and bookings, analyse their sales, draft their contracts for a lawyer to check, and prepare their accounts for their accountant — work that once meant hiring several people or several agencies.

**A teacher** can create personalised practice materials for thirty students at thirty different levels, in the time it used to take to make one worksheet.

**A tradesperson** can run the admin of a five-person business on their own, and spend their evenings with their family instead of their paperwork.

**A nurse or care worker** can get their notes done faster and spend more time with patients.

**A job-seeker** can research a hundred companies, tailor applications to each, and practise interviews with an AI that plays the toughest interviewer they'll meet.

**A creator** can write, design, edit and publish a book, a course, a video series or a newsletter — with a production quality that used to need a team.

**An experienced professional** can take everything they know — twenty years of expertise — and turn it into a consultancy, a training program, a guide or a tool that reaches far more people than they could one-on-one.

**A parent** can run the family's admin, meal plans, budget and schedules with less stress, and get help teaching their kids things they've forgotten themselves.

The thread through all of these: **the human brings the judgement, the relationships, the knowledge of what matters and the responsibility. AI brings the speed and the scale.**

---

### Managing agents: the new management skill

As AI agents get better (Chapter 4), directing will increasingly mean managing a *team* of AI agents — each doing a piece of a bigger job.

It turns out that managing agents is a lot like managing people:

- **Break the work into clear pieces.** Agents do better with well-defined tasks.
- **Give each piece clear instructions and a clear definition of "done."**
- **Check in** — review outputs at key points rather than only at the end.
- **Build in checkpoints before anything consequential happens** — money moving, messages going out, decisions being made.
- **Keep a record** of what was done and why.
- **Take responsibility** for the result.

If you've ever managed people, supervised a team, run a kitchen, coordinated a building site or organised a family holiday for twelve people, you already have more of this skill than you think.

And if you haven't, start small. One agent, one task, carefully checked. Build from there.

---

### The mindset shift

Here's the deepest change this chapter asks for.

Many of us have built our sense of worth around *doing*. "I'm a good writer." "I'm fast with numbers." "I know the system inside out." When a machine can do those things, it can feel like a loss of identity.

I understand that. It's real, and it's worth grieving a little.

But I'd invite you to think of it differently. The *doing* was never really the point. It was the way you delivered value: solving a problem, helping a person, making something work, making something good. AI changes the way you deliver that value. It doesn't change the value itself.

The person who used to write the reports becomes the person who decides what the reports need to say and makes sure they're right. The person who used to do the data entry becomes the person who runs the system and catches the errors. The person who used to know all the answers becomes the person who knows which answers to trust.

That's not a smaller job. In many ways, it's a bigger one.

> **Your value was never your typing speed. It was your judgement, your care, your knowledge of what matters, and your willingness to be responsible. Those are exactly the things AI needs a human for.**

# Glossary

Plain-English definitions of terms used in this book.

**Agent (AI agent)** — An AI that doesn't just answer questions but carries out tasks: taking a goal, breaking it into steps and doing them — browsing, clicking, typing, filling in forms, writing and running code — often checking its own work. (Chapter 2, 4.)

**AI (artificial intelligence)** — Computer systems that perform tasks we'd normally associate with human intelligence: understanding language, recognising images, making predictions, solving problems.

**A-A-A Test** — This book's method for sorting your job's tasks into **Automate** (AI can do it), **Assist** (AI helps you do it) and **Anchor** (needs a human). (Chapter 6.)

**Augmentation** — When AI helps a person do their work better or faster, rather than doing it instead of them. Contrast with **automation**.

**Automation** — When a machine or AI does a task instead of a person.

**Chatbot** — An AI you talk to by typing or speaking, which replies in conversation.

**CRAFT** — This book's method for writing good instructions to AI: **Context, Role, Ask, Format, Test.** (Chapter 14.)

**Data centre** — A large building full of computers that run AI systems and other online services. Uses a lot of electricity and cooling.

**Deepfake** — Fake video, audio or images made with AI that realistically show a real person saying or doing something they never said or did.

**Doubling Clock** — This book's name for the finding that the length of task AI can complete on its own has been doubling every several months. (Chapter 3.)

**Exposure** — How much of a job's tasks could be done or significantly changed by AI. High exposure doesn't necessarily mean the job disappears.

**Five Moats** — The five things that make a task hard for AI to take: **Hands, Heart, Trust, Taste, Accountability.** (Chapter 6.)

**Forecast Labels** — This book's system for marking how certain a claim is: **NOW, LIKELY, POSSIBLE, WILD CARD.**

**Frontier model** — The most advanced AI systems available at any given time.

**Generative AI** — AI that creates new content — text, images, audio, video, code — rather than only analysing existing content.

**Hallucination** — When an AI produces information that sounds right but is false or made up.

**Human Premium** — This book's term for the extra value people place on things done by real humans, as machine-made versions become cheap and abundant. (Chapter 21.)

**Humanoid robot** — A robot built roughly in the shape of a human, designed to work in spaces made for people.

**Income Tripod** — This book's framework for household resilience: **main income, second income, cash cushion.** (Chapter 12.)

**Jagged Edge (jagged frontier)** — The finding that AI is excellent at some tasks and poor at others that seem similar, without warning you which is which. (Chapter 3.)

**Large language model (LLM)** — The type of AI behind most modern chatbots and assistants, trained on huge amounts of text to predict what comes next, which lets it write, answer questions, summarise and more.

**Lazy/Smart Fork** — This book's term for the choice between using AI to think *more* (the smart path) or to think *less* (the lazy path). (Chapter 19.)

**Liar's dividend** — When the existence of deepfakes lets people dismiss *real* evidence as fake.

**Multimodal** — AI that can work with more than one kind of input or output: text, images, audio, video.

**No-code** — Tools that let you build apps or automations without writing programming code.

**Prompt** — The instruction or question you give an AI.

**Proof Over Paper** — This book's principle that real, visible results and projects increasingly matter more than certificates and CVs. (Chapter 11.)

**Robotaxi** — A taxi with no human driver.

**Safe word (family)** — A secret word or phrase agreed within a family to verify identity in an emergency call, protecting against voice-clone scams. (Chapter 16.)

**Sextortion** — Blackmail using real or fake intimate images, threatening to share them unless the victim pays or complies.

**Sycophancy** — An AI's tendency to agree with or flatter the user rather than challenge them.

**Time horizon** — In METR's research, the length of task (measured in human expert time) that an AI can complete successfully about half the time. (Chapter 3.)

**Two-Source Rule** — This book's rule: if it matters, confirm AI-provided information with two independent, reliable non-AI sources. (Chapter 14.)

**Universal Basic Income (UBI)** — A proposal for governments to give everyone regular, unconditional cash payments.

**Vibe coding** — Building software by describing what you want to an AI in plain language and letting it write the code.

**Voice cloning** — Using AI to create a synthetic copy of someone's voice from recordings.
